The Denial Machine — Medicare Advantage Clawed Back $48 Billion From Hospitals in 2025 and Earnings Models Have Not Moved

Medicare Advantage plans denied 10.1 percent of initial claims in 2025 while hospitals permanently lost $48.4 billion in revenue and spent another $43 billion fighting to recover it; the earnings models for for-profit hospital operators have not priced this as a structural cost.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  June 28, 2026  ·  8:00 AM ET

The Number That Does Not Appear in the Earnings Model

Medicare Advantage now covers more than 55 percent of everyone enrolled in Medicare. That is 35 million Americans. The plans advertised zero-premium coverage, dental benefits, and vision care. Enrollment grew fast.

The plans stopped being profitable.

UnitedHealthcare, Humana, and CVS’s Aetna each reported that Medicare Advantage medical costs climbed sharply in 2022 and 2023 as patients returned for procedures they had deferred during the pandemic. By 2024, MA plans collectively lost money on a segment that had been a core growth driver. Management teams on earnings calls described “elevated utilization” as the primary problem.

Their solution was not to raise premiums. That takes a year to implement through CMS’s annual bidding process. The faster option was to deny claims.

Medicare Advantage plans issued 53 million prior authorization determinations in 2024, according to the Kaiser Family Foundation. Initial denial rates in MA averaged 10.1 percent across all claim types. Traditional Medicare’s initial denial rate was 4.2 percent. The gap is not explained by patient acuity. It is explained by the financial position of the plans.

How a Coverage Denial Becomes a Revenue Problem

When a Medicare Advantage plan denies a prior authorization request, the hospital or physician has two choices: accept the denial and lose the revenue, or appeal. Nationally, 57 percent of appealed MA denials are overturned in favor of the provider. That sounds like good news. It is not.

The appeal process takes weeks to months. Staff time and legal resources go into documentation, correspondence, and peer-to-peer reviews. Vendors focused specifically on denial management have grown into a $4 billion industry since 2020. Hospitals pay these vendors to recover revenue from payers. The payer that denied the claim often owns a revenue cycle management subsidiary also available to help the hospital, for a fee.

The American Hospital Association estimates hospitals spend $43 billion annually fighting claim denials. Kodiak Solutions, which tracks revenue cycle data across 2,300 hospitals, reported that net revenue leakage reached $48.4 billion in 2025, up 25 percent from $38.6 billion in 2024.

Hospitals permanently lost $48.4 billion in 2025 because payers denied claims that were never successfully appealed. That figure does not include the $43 billion in administrative costs incurred fighting the denials. Together, the total friction from prior authorization and denial activity approaches $90 billion annually on the hospital side alone.

Post-acute care is the sharpest point. UnitedHealthcare denied 66 percent of initial prior authorization requests for inpatient rehabilitation facilities, according to federal data. Humana denied 54 percent. These are not elective services. Inpatient rehabilitation follows strokes, joint replacements, and major cardiac surgery. Denying them means denying post-surgical care to Medicare-eligible patients until the hospital files an appeal, which takes time and staff the patient does not have.

Who Gets Forced

Community Health Systems carries the sharpest exposure. CHS operates 71 hospitals concentrated in rural and smaller suburban markets, where Medicare and Medicare Advantage make up a disproportionate share of payer mix. In Q1 2026, CHS’s adjusted EBITDA fell 17.8 percent year-over-year. Management cited managed care behavior that had become “more restrictive,” with payers having “turned the dial up on denying pre-authorizations.” They specifically flagged softness in elective procedures including hips and knees. Those are exactly the post-surgical categories where MA denial rates run highest. CHS carries $12.1 billion in debt. A 17.8 percent EBITDA decline at that leverage is not a soft headwind. It is a solvency sequence.

HCA Healthcare is a larger organization with more negotiating power, but HCA’s CFO described denial and underpayment activity as “still really high” after years of investing in revenue cycle resources, technology, and payer partnerships. HCA is not a distress story. It is a margin story: overhead that does not produce care does not produce returns.

Tenet Healthcare faces a simultaneous $250 million 2026 EBITDA headwind from the expiration of enhanced ACA marketplace premium tax credits, which reduced the insured share of its patient population. Adding denial pressure on top of subsidy loss compresses margin from two directions at once.

Twenty-three health systems dropped Medicare Advantage plans entirely in 2026, citing administrative burden and persistent underpayment rates as uneconomic. When a health system drops MA, patients must switch back to traditional Medicare or find a different hospital. Neither outcome helps the operator near term.

The Failure Path

The sequence runs as follows. MA plans continue increasing denial rates through 2026 as they try to restore medical loss ratios after 2023 and 2024 losses. Hospital operators respond by investing in denial-fighting infrastructure, adding to overhead without adding to capacity. Revenue cycle management costs rise. Margins compress further.

Smaller hospitals cannot afford the appeal infrastructure. They write off denied claims faster than large systems. Permanent revenue leakage grows faster at smaller operators. Some exit specific service lines including orthopedics and rehabilitation that generate high denial friction relative to reimbursement. Removing those service lines eliminates the highest-margin procedures from the revenue mix.

CHS is the most visible pressure point. The company’s debt load requires consistent EBITDA performance to service approximately $600 million in annual interest expense. A second consecutive quarter of 17 percent EBITDA declines would put the full-year 2026 guidance range of $1.34 to $1.49 billion in serious doubt. CHS bonds already trade at yields that reflect that concern.

At the system level, the hospitals that cannot absorb denial friction will stop offering the service lines that trigger it. That is a care access problem before it is an equity market problem. The equity repricing of hospital operators comes first.

What I’m Watching

Three numbers carry most of the signal. First, CHS’s Q2 2026 EBITDA. The company reaffirmed full-year guidance of $1.34 to $1.49 billion after a Q1 in which EBITDA fell 17.8 percent year-over-year. Hitting that range requires a meaningful recovery across the remaining three quarters. If Q2 is also soft, the annual range becomes unreachable and the debt service math tightens visibly.

Second, CMS’s prior authorization final rulemaking enforcement timeline. The agency proposed requirements that MA plans must issue prior authorization decisions within 72 hours for urgent requests and seven days for standard requests. The timeline for enforcement matters more than the text of the rule. Delayed enforcement keeps the current incentive structure for denial intact.

Third, the pace of health systems dropping MA plans. At 23 systems in 2026, the departures are significant but not yet systemic. If that number reaches 50 or more, the MA market in affected regions starts to look like a coverage product without a viable provider network, at which point regulatory pressure accelerates.

How This Thesis Fails

The stabilization case rests on CMS enforcement. If the prior authorization reform rules are implemented with real teeth, MA plans would be required to approve more claims at the initial stage. A decline in initial denial rates from 10.1 percent toward 6 or 7 percent would cut administrative friction roughly in half, reducing the $43 billion overhead burden on hospitals immediately.

Market structure also pushes back. As health systems drop MA plans, the remaining provider network shrinks. MA plans need hospitals to retain enrollees. When major systems exit, plans face pressure to improve contract terms to bring them back. That negotiating dynamic could restore hospital reimbursement rates without regulatory action.

There is also evidence the denial wave was partially a response to the 2022 to 2024 cost spike rather than a permanent strategy. Humana’s insurance segment benefit ratio fell from 89.3 percent in Q1 2024 to 87.4 percent in Q1 2025, indicating that MA plans are beginning to restore profitability without relying entirely on denial volume. If medical loss ratios normalize organically, the pressure to deny claims eases and approval rates recover.

Closing Thoughts

Hospitals lost $48.4 billion in 2025 to denied and uncollectable claims, up $9.8 billion from the year before. The number grows every year.

The earnings models for hospital operators do not price this as a structural cost. They should.

Plain English

Even though Medicare is supposed to pay hospitals for the care they deliver to patients over 65, the private companies running Medicare Advantage plans have been systematically rejecting claims for covered services at a rate of about 10 out of every 100 requests. I argue this because the data from 2025 shows that hospitals permanently lost $48 billion in revenue they were already owed, and had to spend another $43 billion just fighting to get some of it back. Although some of these denials eventually get overturned when hospitals appeal, the process takes months and costs enormous administrative resources that smaller hospitals simply cannot afford. You may start seeing effects if you own hospital company stocks, particularly smaller chains like Community Health Systems, whose profits fell nearly 18 percent in the first quarter of 2026 as payers, for now, keep turning up the pressure on what they approve.
