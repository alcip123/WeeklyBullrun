The Enrichment Gap — America’s Nuclear Revival Runs on Russian Fuel It Has 17 Months to Replace

Tech companies are signing nuclear power deals by the gigawatt. The enrichment capacity to fuel those reactors does not exist in the United States.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  August 14, 2026  ·  1:22 PM ET

The Tech Deals Are Real. The Fuel Isn’t.

Meta signed agreements for up to 7.8 gigawatts of nuclear capacity this year. Microsoft committed to more than 800 megawatts for its data center operations. Constellation Energy, the largest operator of US nuclear reactors, signed 920 megawatts of new long-term contracts in the second quarter of 2026 alone and raised its full-year earnings guidance to $11.50 to $12.50 per share. Nuclear energy has become the AI industry’s power solution of choice: the only carbon-free source that can deliver electricity around the clock without depending on weather.

The premise of every one of those contracts is that the reactors will have fuel. According to the Energy Information Administration’s 2024 Uranium Marketing Annual Report, US reactor operators purchased 3.28 million separative work units of uranium enrichment services from Russia in 2025, accounting for 26% of all US enrichment purchases. A decade ago, Russia’s share was 17%. The dependency has grown, not shrunk.

In 17 months, that supply is legally cut off.

The Step Between the Mine and the Reactor

Uranium cannot travel directly from a mine to a reactor. The ore is first converted to a gaseous form, then run through a plant that concentrates the fissile U-235 isotope from its natural abundance of 0.7% to the 3 to 5% required for a commercial reactor fuel rod. That concentration step is called enrichment, and it is measured in separative work units, or SWU. The process is technically demanding, capital-intensive, and controlled by fewer than a dozen facilities worldwide.

US reactors consumed roughly 12.6 million SWU of enrichment services in 2025. At an average contract price of $108.70 per SWU, that is a market of approximately $1.4 billion per year in enrichment alone, before conversion and fuel fabrication costs. The facilities producing those SWU are divided among France’s Orano, the British-Dutch-German consortium Urenco, Russian state company Rosatom, and a small number of others. Centrus Energy, the only company with a US-government license for commercial uranium enrichment, currently operates a demonstration cascade in Piketon, Ohio with output far below commercial scale.

Rosatom’s 26% share is not a rounding error. It is the largest single-country foreign source of US enrichment supply, ahead of France at 18%, the United Kingdom at 14%, and the Netherlands at 8%.

The Utility Caught Between the Contract and the Cost

The power purchase agreements that tech companies sign do not expose them to fuel cost risk. When Microsoft or Meta buys electricity from a nuclear utility at a fixed price per megawatt-hour over ten or twenty years, the fuel cost exposure stays with the utility. If enrichment costs rise, the utility absorbs the margin compression.

Constellation Energy’s nuclear fuel costs for 2026 are guided at $5.75 to $5.80 per megawatt-hour, up 9% from the $5.30 to $5.35 range in 2025. That increase reflects rising uranium and enrichment contract prices before any supply disruption. Constellation operates the largest unregulated nuclear fleet in the United States, and it does not have a fuel cost pass-through mechanism on that merchant generation. Every dollar of enrichment cost increase comes directly out of margins that are already committed to fixed-price customers at locked rates.

Vistra Energy, the second-largest US nuclear operator, is in the same structural position. Both companies are priced by the market as pure-play beneficiaries of AI power demand. Neither has publicly disclosed what share of its enrichment supply is contracted from non-Russian sources through the late 2020s.

The contracts are signed. The fuel supply behind them is not fully secured.

The Law Takes Effect in January 2028. New Capacity Arrives in 2029.

The Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act, signed in May 2024, bans imports of Russian low-enriched uranium into the United States beginning January 1, 2028. That is 17 months from today.

The federal response was substantial. In January 2026, the Department of Energy awarded $2.7 billion in enrichment contracts across three companies: $900 million to Centrus Energy to expand its Piketon, Ohio facility; $900 million to France’s Orano; and $900 million to new entrant General Matter. Centrus subsequently engaged Fluor as its engineering and construction contractor for the Ohio expansion, with commercial-scale operations targeted for 2029. European enrichers are also expanding, but nuclear enrichment capacity cannot be built on a short timeline. Centrifuge manufacturing is a specialized discipline. Regulatory approvals alone take years.

The arithmetic does not close in 2028. Russia supplies 3.28 million SWU annually to US reactors. The DOE-funded expansion capacity will not be operational before 2029, a full year after the Russian supply is legally cut. What fills the 2028 gap is not clearly answered anywhere in the public record.

The law moved faster than the supply chain.

Three Signals That Reveal How Tight This Gets

Separative work unit spot prices are the earliest indicator. Long-term enrichment contracts already reflect some tightness: the average US utility paid $108.70 per SWU in 2025, up sharply from prior years as uranium prices rose 167% following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in February 2022. Spot SWU prices move faster than contracted prices when buyers compete for constrained supply. When utilities begin locking down non-Russian enrichment contracts in 2027, spot prices will signal the shortage before any physical disruption is visible.

The second signal is Centrus’s construction milestones at Piketon. The Centrus-Fluor partnership was signed in February 2026, with commercial operations targeted for 2029. Nuclear construction projects routinely run behind schedule. Any announced delay extends the gap between the January 2028 Russian ban and the arrival of domestic replacement capacity.

The third signal is DOE waiver filings. The Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act permits the Department of Energy to grant waivers for utilities that cannot source non-Russian enrichment. When waiver applications begin arriving in 2027, the number and volume covered will reveal which utilities have failed to secure alternative supply. The waivers, when they come, will be the clearest available signal of how wide the gap actually is.

What Would Prove This Wrong

The case breaks if Urenco executes its expansion plans faster than current projections. Urenco is the largest non-Russian enricher operating in the Western world, with facilities in the UK, Netherlands, Germany, and a US plant in New Mexico. It already supplies a significant share of American utility needs. Faster capacity additions from Urenco would reduce the volume that must come from new DOE-funded sources in 2028 and 2029.

It also breaks if US utilities have already contracted sufficient non-Russian enrichment through 2030 at fixed prices. Some utilities do disclose long-term enrichment contract coverage in their annual reports. If the industry has quietly hedged its Russian exposure in advance, the supply cliff exists on paper but not in practice. That information is not currently visible in aggregate form.

Finally, the thesis breaks if the US government grants broad DOE waivers that effectively extend Russian import permissions past January 2028 on national security grounds. The current administration has shown flexibility on similar trade restrictions in other sectors. If waivers are granted at scale, the hard deadline becomes elastic, and the pricing pressure on enrichment shifts further into the future.

A Dependency Built Over Decades Has 17 Months to Unwind

Nuclear energy’s moment is real. The demand from AI infrastructure is real. The equity valuations on the companies building that future are priced accordingly.

The Russian enrichment contracts disappear on a fixed legal date in January 2028. The supply chain being built to replace them arrives in 2029.

Plain English

Even though nuclear energy is being called the clean, reliable power source that will fuel the AI era, the companies signing those power deals still depend significantly on a Russian government-controlled company to process the uranium those reactors run on. I argue this because turning raw uranium into reactor fuel requires a separate industrial step called enrichment, and Russia currently handles 26% of all the enrichment that American nuclear plants use, a share that has actually grown over the past decade. Although a federal law banning that Russian supply takes effect in January 2028, the American and European facilities being built to replace it won’t be ready until 2029, leaving roughly a year where a large portion of the fuel supply has no operational replacement in place. If that gap pushes electricity costs higher, you may find that the clean energy future being built for AI data centers comes with a higher power bill than the companies signing those deals are currently advertising.
