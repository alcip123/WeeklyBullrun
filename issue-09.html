The Medicaid Enrollment Cliff — 7.6 Million Exits Are Already Law and Managed Care Has Not Priced Them

Medicaid managed care companies are reporting better margins while losing hundreds of thousands of members. The law requires millions more to go in January, all at once.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  August 25, 2026  ·  9:38 AM ET

Why Centene Raised Guidance While Losing 700,000 Medicaid Members

Centene reported $1.1 billion in second-quarter profit on July 28 and raised its full-year earnings guidance. In the same filing, it disclosed that Medicaid membership had fallen from 12.8 million to 12.1 million over the past year. Those two facts are not in tension. They describe the same mechanism viewed from two different angles.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed into law on July 4, 2025, imposed the largest reduction in federal Medicaid spending since the program's creation in 1965. The Congressional Budget Office projects 7.6 million people will ultimately lose Medicaid coverage under its provisions. Implementation is phased. The most consequential provision, work requirements, does not take effect until January 1, 2027.

The enrollment declines so far are the early wave. The 3.8 percent membership drop across the five largest Medicaid managed care organizations since the law's passage reflects accelerated redeterminations and administrative eligibility restrictions already in force. The bigger hit is scheduled. Markets have not priced it.

The Capitation Math That Breaks When Members Leave Faster Than Costs

Medicaid managed care operates on fixed monthly payments per enrolled member, set by state contracts. Each state pays a per-member-per-month capitation rate. Revenue scales directly with enrollment. Costs do not.

Administrative infrastructure, including claims processing systems, compliance operations, network management, and provider credentialing, adjusts slowly. A company managing 12.1 million Medicaid members carries nearly the same overhead as one managing 12.8 million. The margin improvement Centene has posted this year stems partly from shedding relatively high-cost members during redeterminations and partly from repricing in other business lines. Its Medicaid-specific health benefits ratio remains at 93.9 percent in Q2 2026, with a full-year target near 93.5 percent.

Molina Healthcare projects a full-year Medicaid pretax margin of 1.2 percent on approximately $42 billion in annualized premium revenue. That equates to roughly $500 million in Medicaid profit before taxes. A fixed cost base that does not contract proportionally with membership turns a 10 percent enrollment decline into a margin event that the headline pretax rate does not telegraph in advance.

The January 2027 provision introduces a categorical shock. Adults between 19 and 64 enrolled through ACA Medicaid expansion must document 80 hours per month of work, school, or community service or lose coverage. The CBO projects 2.3 million people will exit Medicaid in the first year of implementation, rising to between 3.1 and 3.3 million in subsequent years. First disenrollments are scheduled for April 1, 2027. States must finalize their implementation plans by December 31, 2026.

The Two Plans That Run on Medicaid Alone

Molina Healthcare derives approximately 80 percent of its total premium revenue from Medicaid. Centene derives approximately 60 percent. Both companies earn their margins from state managed care contracts, which pay a fixed rate per member in exchange for bearing the financial risk of that member's healthcare costs.

Since the law's passage, the five largest Medicaid managed care organizations have collectively lost 1.4 million members, a 3.8 percent decline from 36.2 million to 34.8 million as of Q2 2026. Centene is guiding to an 8 to 9 percent full-year membership reduction. Molina has seen a 5.8 percent enrollment decline in the months since enactment.

Hospitals face the parallel side of the same mechanism. Premier Health estimates $68.5 billion in hospital revenue is at risk through 2026 and 2027. The Commonwealth Fund projects operating margins at safety-net hospitals will fall from 3.9 percent to 1.7 percent as coverage losses flow through to uncompensated care. Uncompensated care costs are projected to rise by $2.2 billion as newly uninsured patients present at emergency departments without the ability to pay. Rural facilities face a 35.4 percent average increase in uncompensated costs. More than 150 rural hospitals have closed or converted to non-acute care since 2010.

When the State Cannot Fund a Higher Per-Member Rate

The standard managed care response to enrollment loss is contract renegotiation: fewer members, higher per-member payments to offset the fixed cost dilution. States are losing the financial mechanism they have historically used to fund those negotiations.

The law restricts provider taxes, which states have used to generate federal matching funds and route the combined proceeds back to Medicaid managed care plans as supplemental per-member payments. Managed care organization provider taxes must unwind by the end of state fiscal years ending in 2026. Hospital provider taxes are capped through 2028, then reduced by 0.5 percentage points per year, reaching a final threshold of 3.5 percent in 2032, down from rates approaching 6 percent in several states today.

Illinois projects annual federal Medicaid reductions of $2.8 billion by fiscal year 2031. Connecticut's governor proposed cutting hospital provider taxes by $275 million as part of the state's budget adjustment to the new federal framework. Multiply those figures across approximately 40 states operating Medicaid managed care programs and the aggregate revenue pressure on plans is structural rather than episodic.

When states cannot fund higher rates and plans cannot absorb margin compression on 1.2 percent pretax margins, the arithmetic resolves through network contraction. Plans restrict provider participation. Providers exit networks. Safety-net hospitals, which operate at median margins below 1 percent, cannot sustain uncompensated care increases without cutting service lines or closing entirely.

State Implementation Plans Due December 31

The December 31, 2026 deadline for state implementation plans is the first hard signal of how aggressively the April enrollment cliff will materialize. States that submit detailed, operational plans by that date accelerate the Q1 2027 disenrollment wave. States that delay or challenge the requirements in court push it into 2028 and beyond.

Three other signals matter. Centene and Molina report Q3 2026 earnings in October and will include updated full-year membership guidance and first commentary on 2027 trajectories. Any upward revision to Centene's 8 to 9 percent decline estimate signals acceleration. Per-member-per-month rate announcements from states finalizing 2027 managed care contracts are the direct indicator of whether plans will absorb the fixed cost pressure or push it back through network restrictions. Hospital uncompensated care disclosures in Q4 earnings, particularly from chains with significant safety-net exposure, will show whether the newly uninsured are already arriving before the January cutoff formally begins.

If Courts Block the Requirements the Way They Did in 2019

When the first Trump administration attempted Medicaid work requirements through state waivers between 2018 and 2019, federal courts blocked implementation in Arkansas, Kentucky, and Indiana, ruling that the requirements were inconsistent with Medicaid's statutory purpose of providing medical coverage. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act implements work requirements through federal statute rather than state waiver, which changes the legal challenge structurally. Courts have not yet ruled on the statutory version.

A second scenario that breaks the thesis: large Medicaid expansion states absorb provider tax losses from general fund revenue. California, New York, and Illinois have signaled resistance to full federal implementation. States that choose fiscal strain over coverage losses protect managed care enrollment and per-member rates, at the cost of their own balance sheets.

A third: the members who remain after work requirements prove healthier than CBO projections assume. Redetermination processes have already removed a disproportionate share of high-utilization enrollees through administrative exits. If the remaining population uses less care, health benefits ratios improve and 1.2 percent pretax margins expand rather than compress. The thesis assumes actuarial stability in the remaining risk pool. That assumption has not been tested at this scale.

The 2.3 Million Exits Already Scheduled for April

The managed care stocks have not repriced the 2027 enrollment cliff. Centene raised guidance. Molina beat second-quarter estimates. Both companies' margin improvements are real, built on a year of shedding expensive members and repricing remaining business lines.

The mechanism that built those margins is nearly spent. The 2.3 million CBO-projected exits in 2027 are administrative, not actuarial. They are people who may qualify for coverage but cannot satisfy an 80-hour monthly documentation requirement. Fixed cost bases do not contract. A 10 percent enrollment decline on a 1.2 percent pretax margin ends in losses.

Plain English

Even though the big health insurance companies that manage government Medicaid coverage for lower-income people are still reporting strong profits, the number of people they cover is shrinking and a much larger drop is already written into law for early next year. I argue this because a federal law passed last year cuts roughly $1 trillion from Medicaid over the next decade, and starting in January 2027, millions of people will be required to prove they work at least 80 hours a month or lose their health insurance, while the money states use to pay these companies per person covered is also being phased down. Although the insurance companies have managed to improve their margins on remaining members for now, their profit cushion is razor thin. When the January deadline arrives and several million people are dropped from the rolls at once, you may start seeing these companies report losses, hospital emergency rooms fill with uninsured patients, and rural hospitals that depend on Medicaid close their doors.
