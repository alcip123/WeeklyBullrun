The July Blind Spot — Hospital Stocks Are Priced on Q2 Numbers as Two Revenue Streams End

Medicaid work requirements effective July 31 will strip an estimated 7.8 million from coverage just as an $8 billion cut to hospital subsidies begins. Every earnings report published so far reflects none of it.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  August 18, 2026  ·  9:28 AM ET

Why Hospital Earnings Look Strong When the Revenue Math Is About to Break

The second quarter was good for hospital companies. Tenet Healthcare raised its 2026 guidance after its hospital segment beat consensus EBITDA by more than 14 percent. HCA Healthcare recognized $400 million in Medicaid supplemental payment revenue. Community Health Systems posted an EBITDA margin of 11.7 percent. By the numbers visible to markets, the sector is stable.

It isn't. Q2 results reflect care delivered between April 1 and June 30. Two structural cuts to hospital Medicaid revenue took effect in July, after those earnings windows closed. Neither has appeared in a quarterly report yet.

The cuts are the Medicaid work requirements that took effect July 31 under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, and a reduction of $8 billion in disproportionate share hospital payments in fiscal year 2026. These are not the same cut. They hit hospital revenue through different channels. They land simultaneously.

Medicaid Revenue Runs on Two Tracks. Both Are Being Cut.

Safety-net hospitals depend on Medicaid through two distinct revenue streams. The first is fee-for-service reimbursement: each time a Medicaid enrollee receives care, the hospital submits a claim and receives payment. That payment already covers only about 83 cents on the dollar of actual hospital costs, according to the American Hospital Association. Hospitals treat Medicaid patients at a structured loss from the start.

The second stream is disproportionate share hospital payments, known as DSH: direct federal transfers to hospitals that serve high proportions of Medicaid and uninsured patients. DSH exists because patient-level Medicaid reimbursement is insufficient to cover costs. It is a subsidy designed to compensate for that gap. It has been in place for decades and is embedded in the financial models of every safety-net system in the country.

Both are being cut. The Congressional Budget Office projects that the OBBBA's work requirements will strip 7.8 million people of Medicaid coverage. CMS estimates 2.3 million disenrollments specifically in fiscal year 2027. When those patients seek care, which they will because illness does not check employment status, they arrive uninsured. Federal law requires hospitals to stabilize any patient who shows up. They cannot bill a payer. That cost becomes uncompensated care, absorbed directly into the hospital's operating loss.

The DSH cut removes $8 billion in FY2026 and $24 billion over three years. Congress designed DSH to compensate hospitals for exactly this situation. It is being reduced as the situation gets worse.

The Rural Hospital With No Fallback

More than 40 percent of rural hospitals in the United States are currently operating at a loss. Chartis identified 417 rural facilities as vulnerable to closure. These hospitals typically have Medicaid as their largest single payer, often exceeding 30 percent of total revenue. They have no commercial patient mix to cross-subsidize Medicaid losses. They cannot renegotiate with large insurers the way urban systems can. They cannot pursue outpatient clinic acquisitions or physician practice buyouts to diversify their revenue base.

The Commonwealth Fund calculated that Medicaid work requirements will reduce operating margins by an average of 13.3 percent for hospitals in expansion states. For safety-net hospitals specifically, the reduction is 29.6 percent. Fitch currently forecasts the median operating margin for nonprofit hospitals at 1 to 2 percent in 2026. A 29.6 percent reduction in a 2 percent margin is not compression. It is elimination.

Public Citizen's analysis identified 446 hospitals nationally as at high risk of closing or cutting services. Premier, the hospital purchasing cooperative, estimates the sector will lose $68.6 billion in revenue across 2026 and 2027 from rising uninsurance tied to Medicaid and marketplace changes. Site-neutral Medicare payment policies are running concurrently: they will cost rural hospitals an additional $1.7 billion in 2026 by equalizing reimbursement between hospital outpatient departments and independent physician clinics. Rural hospitals built their financial models assuming they would receive the hospital rate for outpatient services. They will not.

When Q3 Earnings Show What July Did

Third quarter earnings from hospital operators come out in October. That is the first public window into payer mix data from July, August, and September. HCA Healthcare and Community Health Systems both lowered their full-year guidance in Q2, citing higher-than-expected uninsured volumes. They were measuring what happened in January through June. July had not started.

Community Health Systems is the most exposed publicly traded hospital operator. Analysts project revenues declining roughly 1.7 percent annually with EBITDA margins moving from 3.8 percent toward breakeven. CYH carries a significant long-term debt load, meaning small shifts in payer mix have an amplified effect on interest coverage. The Q2 earnings miss was not an isolated quarter. The conditions it reflects are structurally worse in Q3.

Medical Properties Trust owns 373 hospital properties and approximately 38,000 licensed beds across 51 hospital operating companies, making it the largest healthcare REIT with direct hospital exposure. MPT is still recovering from the bankruptcies of Steward Health Care and Prospect Medical, both of which executed sale-leaseback transactions with MPT and then collapsed, leaving MPT holding properties it needed to re-tenant at a loss. MPT is targeting $1 billion in annualized cash rent by year-end 2026. If hospital tenants request payment deferrals in the second half of the year, that target is at risk. MPT's stock is priced for a full recovery. A second wave of tenant stress is not in that price.

Three Numbers That Will Tell the Story First

The first is the uninsured patient admission rate in Q3 earnings. Watch Community Health Systems, HCA, and Tenet. Both CYH and HCA flagged above-forecast uninsured volumes in Q2 already. Any uptick from Q2 levels will confirm that July disenrollments are reaching actual care volumes.

The second is state DSH allotment data from CMS in August and September. The $8 billion FY2026 reduction flows through state-level allotments. States with high concentrations of safety-net hospitals, Texas, Florida, and New York, will absorb the sharpest reductions. When CMS distributes revised allotment figures, the per-hospital impact becomes calculable.

The third is MPT's Q3 rent collection disclosures. In prior earnings calls, MPT has broken out billed rent versus cash collected for transition tenants. Any new deferrals, restructurings, or collection shortfalls will appear in that data before they show up anywhere else.

The Supplemental Payment Buffer Has a Ceiling

Tenet and HCA both demonstrated in Q2 that state Medicaid supplemental payment programs can cushion the blow. HCA's $400 million in supplemental payments offset what would otherwise have been a materially worse result. These programs exist across most expansion states and allow state Medicaid agencies to direct additional funds to hospitals without increasing federal matching requirements above certain thresholds. For large for-profit systems with the contracting relationships to access these programs, they work.

The buffer has limits. Supplemental payments require state funding matches. As more patients arrive uninsured and DSH allotments fall, states face the same fiscal pressure hospitals do. Safety-net hospitals that serve the most exposed patient populations often have the weakest relationships with state Medicaid agencies and the least ability to negotiate supplemental payment arrangements.

Congressional action could delay the DSH cuts again. There is bipartisan precedent: a prior bill attracted support from both parties to push back earlier rounds of cuts. If that happens, the hospital revenue picture improves in the near term. But it does not reverse the payer mix shift from work requirement disenrollments. Work requirements took effect July 31. The patients who lost coverage do not regain it automatically even if DSH cuts are deferred.

The Quarter That Reflects July Has Not Been Reported Yet

Hospital financial reporting is quarterly. Medicaid policy changes take effect the day Congress says they do. The work requirements started July 31. The DSH cut runs through the current federal fiscal year, which began in October 2025. Neither shows up in any earnings disclosure markets have seen.

The Q3 reports in October are the first honest read. Until then, hospital stocks and healthcare REITs are priced on June data.

Plain English

Even though major hospital companies just posted some of their strongest quarterly earnings in years, the rules that determine how hospitals get paid for treating low-income patients changed significantly at the end of July. I argue this because Congress cut a direct federal payment that hospitals have long relied on to cover the cost of treating uninsured patients, while also introducing new requirements that will push millions of people off Medicaid coverage and turn them into patients hospitals are legally required to treat for free. Although hospital finances have looked stable so far this year, those results cover April through June, before any of the new rules took effect. When the next round of quarterly results comes out in October, you may start seeing hospitals report weaker numbers, which could affect the companies that own hospital properties, the hospital chains that already operate on razor-thin margins, and the medical suppliers that depend on hospitals keeping their purchasing budgets intact.
