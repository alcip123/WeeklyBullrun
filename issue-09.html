Three Million Dropped Coverage — The Tab Is Landing on Community Hospitals

The expiration of enhanced ACA marketplace subsidies has pushed nearly three million Americans off their insurance plans, and the earnings of community hospital chains are now showing exactly where those people ended up.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  September 22, 2026  ·  9:21 AM ET

Hospital Admissions Are Rising. Revenue Per Admission Is Not.

Community Health Systems treated more patients in the second quarter of 2026 than it did a year earlier. Same-store adjusted admissions grew 2.9 percent year over year. By normal logic, more patients means more revenue.

It doesn't. Adjusted EBITDA fell to $330 million from $380 million in Q2 2025, a $50 million compression in a single quarter. Uncompensated care as a percentage of adjusted net revenue climbed from 26.6 percent to 32.9 percent — 630 basis points of deterioration in twelve months. Roughly half the admission growth came from patients who arrived without insurance. CYH stock fell 12.83 percent on the day the earnings were released.

Management cut full-year revenue guidance to $11.4 billion to $11.6 billion from a prior range of $11.6 billion to $12 billion, and trimmed adjusted EBITDA guidance to $1.30 billion to $1.375 billion. CYH carries approximately $12 billion in outstanding debt. At the lower end of the new guidance range, that leaves almost no room for further deterioration.

This is not a one-company story. Tenet Healthcare reported ACA-related patient volumes down 13.5 percent and revenues from those patients down 17 percent year over year in Q2. HCA Healthcare disclosed that nearly all of its ACA marketplace patients had shifted to uninsured status. The common cause is not company-specific. It is a policy change that took effect on January 1, 2026.

When Premiums Double, People Do Not Shop for Cheaper Plans

Enhanced ACA premium tax credits were first authorized under the 2021 American Rescue Plan and extended through the Inflation Reduction Act. They compressed marketplace premiums to near zero for lower-income enrollees and reduced costs meaningfully for households earning between 100 and 400 percent of the federal poverty line. Congress did not renew them beyond 2025.

The pricing effect was immediate. Average monthly marketplace premiums rose 58 percent, from approximately $113 to $178. For enrollees at the lower end of the subsidy range — earning $30,000 to $35,000 — the effective increase was sharper. At $178 per month, annual health insurance costs $2,136, or roughly 7 percent of a $30,000 income. The Urban Institute projected that 4.8 million additional Americans would become uninsured as a direct result of the expiration.

By February 2026, actual federal enrollment data confirmed the trajectory. ACA marketplace enrollment had fallen from 22.1 million people in 2025 to 19.2 million, a 13 percent decline and the largest single-year drop since the exchanges opened in 2014. Sign-ups declined in 41 states. These were not people who found alternatives. They disenrolled and went without coverage.

Rural and Community Hospitals Absorb the Entire Adjustment

When uninsured patients need care, they do not stop going to hospitals. They stop paying for the care they receive. Under EMTALA, every hospital with an emergency department must evaluate and stabilize any patient regardless of insurance status or ability to pay. The cost of that care moves to the uncompensated care ledger — revenue the hospital provided and will not collect.

The distribution of this burden is not equal. Academic medical centers and large urban systems draw from higher-income catchment areas with stronger payer mixes. A few thousand lower-income marketplace enrollees losing insurance in a major metropolitan area represents a small fraction of their total patient volume.

Community and rural hospitals operate in different markets entirely. Community Health Systems' 71 hospitals sit in smaller cities and rural counties across 13 states — exactly the markets where a doubling of monthly premiums produced the highest disenrollment rates. Molina Healthcare, which manages Medicaid coverage in these same markets, saw its total enrollment fall from 5.7 million members in mid-2025 to 4.9 million by June 2026. The geography of coverage loss and the geography of community hospital exposure are nearly identical.

The Federal Safety Net That Partially Offsets This Has a Scheduled Cut

The Disproportionate Share Hospital program — DSH — compensates hospitals for treating disproportionately high volumes of uninsured and low-income patients. It was designed for exactly the situation now unfolding.

In January 2026 legislation, Congress delayed $8 billion in annual DSH payment cuts until Fiscal Year 2028. Hospital systems had lobbied aggressively to postpone the reductions, and they succeeded. The cuts were pushed out by two years.

The delay was not a cancellation. Starting in 2028, DSH payments face an annual reduction exceeding 50 percent. For hospitals already absorbing elevated uncompensated care from enrollment losses, the reduction arrives as a permanent structural change layered on top of a coverage gap that shows no sign of self-correcting. Congressional action projected to increase hospital uncompensated care costs by $466 billion between 2025 and 2034, according to America's Essential Hospitals. Essential hospitals, which account for 6 percent of acute-care facilities nationally, are expected to bear 25 percent of that added cost.

If Congress Restores the Subsidies, the Margin Problem Reverses Quickly

The thesis rests entirely on the continuation of the coverage gap. A single legislative act extending enhanced premium tax credits would push most disenrolled enrollees back into marketplace coverage within one open-enrollment period. Uncompensated care rates would normalize in the following quarter. Hospital earnings would recover in proportion to the coverage restoration.

That is not an implausible outcome. Enhanced tax credits had bipartisan support in earlier legislative cycles. A budget negotiation or reconciliation process could restore them. Several states are designing their own premium subsidy programs to partially offset federal inaction at the margin.

Tenet's Q2 2026 performance also illustrates a ceiling on the downside. The company raised full-year guidance despite ACA headwinds, absorbing the mix shift through better payer contracting and stronger performance in higher-margin service lines. Larger, geographically diversified systems are not equally exposed. The concentrated risk sits with community hospital operators whose geographic footprint overlaps most heavily with the disenrolled population.

The Coverage Gap Has No Market Mechanism to Close Itself

Nearly three million people left marketplace coverage in twelve months because premiums doubled. At $178 per month, they will not return voluntarily. The hospitals that treated them in Q2 will treat them again in Q3.

The DSH cut arrives in 2028 whether or not the coverage gap has closed.

Plain English

Even though hospitals across the country are seeing more patients walk through their doors in 2026, the earnings of community hospital chains are getting squeezed in a way that investors are only beginning to price in. I argue this because the people showing up to these hospitals in larger numbers are the same people who lost their health insurance when federal subsidies expired at the start of the year, and a hospital that treats an uninsured patient cannot collect meaningful payment for that care. Although patient volumes look strong on the surface, they aren't generating the revenue that the same volumes would have a year ago. If federal policy does not change, you may start seeing more hospital systems reduce services or close departments, well before a second round of federal payment cuts arrives in 2028.
