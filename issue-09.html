The Trade That Broke Treasuries Once — And Is Twice as Large Now

Hedge funds have borrowed more than $1.8 trillion in overnight repo to run a Treasury arbitrage that generates fractions of a percent per trade, and the Fed, the IMF, and the FSB each published warnings about the same forced-unwind risk in 2026.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  September 25, 2026  ·  9:27 AM ET

Why the World’s Safest Market Already Failed Once

The United States Treasury market is the benchmark for every other asset class on earth. At $31 trillion in outstanding debt, it is where pension funds store capital, central banks hold reserves, and mortgage rates find their floor. It is supposed to be the one thing that still works when everything else does not.

For four days in March 2020, it didn’t work. Bid-ask spreads on benchmark 10-year notes widened to roughly 50 times their normal level. Primary dealers, the banks legally obligated to bid at Treasury auctions, pulled back from market-making as their own balance sheets came under pressure. The Federal Reserve responded by injecting $1.7 trillion in emergency repo funding over a single week and purchasing more than $500 billion in Treasuries in less than a month.

The proximate cause was identified quickly: hedge funds unwound a specific arbitrage trade simultaneously, flooding the cash Treasury market with supply that no available buyer could absorb. The crisis passed. The trade grew back.

It is now approximately twice as large as it was then.

How a Trade That Earns Fractions of a Percent Borrows $1.8 Trillion Overnight

The mechanics are straightforward. A hedge fund buys a Treasury bond in the cash market and simultaneously sells a corresponding Treasury futures contract. Futures prices tend to trade slightly above cash prices because of financing costs and delivery optionality. That gap — the basis — is the profit. On any individual bond, the spread is measured in basis points. One basis point is one hundredth of one percent.

To turn fractions of a percent into returns worth running, the trade requires extreme leverage.

A hedge fund finances its Treasury purchase through the repurchase agreement market, posting the bond as collateral and borrowing overnight at a rate close to the federal funds rate. The Financial Stability Board’s February 2026 report on repo market vulnerabilities found that approximately 70% of government bond-backed repo operates with zero haircuts — a fund can post a $100 million Treasury bond and borrow the full $100 million against it with no cushion.

Multiply that across thousands of positions. A Federal Reserve analysis published in June 2026 found that basis trade volumes reached approximately $830 billion, roughly double their previous peak in early 2020. Total gross notional exposure across related strategies ranges from $1 trillion to $2 trillion, according to IMF estimates in the April 2026 Global Financial Stability Report. Hedge funds hold roughly 8% of the $31 trillion Treasury market and depend on more than $6.6 trillion in repo and prime brokerage loans to support those positions. Net hedge fund repo borrowing reached $1.8 trillion by late 2025, equal to 6% of all outstanding marketable Treasury notes and bonds.

Leverage on individual basis positions runs 10 to 20 times capital. The trade is profitable at that leverage. It is dangerous at that leverage too.

The Repo Desk Cuts the Line and Positions Have Nowhere to Go

The basis trade has one structural dependency: daily overnight repo funding. When that funding becomes unavailable, positions must be unwound immediately. There is no gradual exit. The trade has no natural holding period because it is financed day by day. A fund cannot decide to wait out the stress.

The mechanism works as follows. A shock arrives: a Treasury auction that clears badly, a sudden spike in yields, a bank-specific liquidity squeeze, or a broad risk-off shift that causes prime brokers to reassess counterparty exposure. Repo rates spike. Haircuts rise from zero toward 2% or 3%. The cost of financing a $100 million Treasury position overnight increases by tens of thousands of dollars per day.

Some funds absorb the higher cost. Others cannot. When funds begin selling their cash Treasury positions to exit, the Treasury market receives a sudden, large wave of non-economic supply. Treasury prices fall and yields rise. Rising yields increase the unrealized loss on the futures legs of other funds’ positions, triggering additional margin calls. The cascade is reflexive.

This is not a theoretical sequence. The IMF’s April 2026 Global Financial Stability Report named forced deleveraging among leveraged nonbank financial intermediaries as one of the most acute systemic risks in the current environment, explicitly identifying Treasury cash-futures basis strategies as the primary vehicle.

March 2020 Wrote the Script — The Position Has Doubled Since

The 2020 episode is the only complete test on record of what happens when basis trades unwind under stress. When the pandemic shock arrived, hedge funds began unwinding positions at scale. Treasury yields moved 70 basis points within days, a historically extreme move for an asset class treated as riskless. Dealers pulled back from market-making. The Fed intervened with $1.7 trillion in emergency repo and $500 billion in Treasury purchases in under three weeks.

The scale of intervention required to stabilize that unwind is the most important single data point about what happens without a functioning lender of last resort. The Fed had to become the buyer of last resort for the entire Treasury market because no private institution could absorb the selling.

Basis trade positions have since grown to roughly double their 2020 peak. The FSB’s 2026 report confirmed that the same zero-haircut repo structure, the same overnight funding reliance, and the same concentration in a small number of large multi-strategy funds remains in place. The position has grown. The infrastructure supporting it has not.

Two Data Points That Signal Stress Before Markets Price It

Repo rates are the primary early warning. The Secured Overnight Financing Rate, which reflects the rate on Treasury-collateralized overnight repo, normally trades within a few basis points of the federal funds effective rate. Brief spikes of 10 to 15 basis points occurred in April and July 2026. A sustained divergence of more than 15 basis points lasting more than three consecutive days would indicate that repo supply is thinning and prime brokers are cutting back.

Treasury auction results are the second indicator. When bid-to-cover ratios on 10-year note auctions fall below 2.3, or when auction tails — the gap between the clearing yield and the pre-auction when-issued yield — exceed 3 basis points consistently, dealer demand is weakening. Primary dealers absorb what others won’t buy. Thin dealer demand is the direct precursor to a market-making withdrawal under stress.

Both metrics are publicly available in real time. Neither receives much attention in mainstream financial coverage.

When the Spread Disappears and the Trade Exits Quietly

The basis trade has already been shrinking. Positions fell from approximately $1.26 trillion at the start of 2026 to roughly $900 billion by September, the smallest estimated level in more than two years. The driver was compression in the spread itself: as more capital chased the same arbitrage, the basis narrowed and the risk-adjusted return fell. Funds reduced positions without a crisis forcing them out.

Organic compression is the benign scenario. If the basis continues to narrow because competition has driven out the excess return, positions exit gradually, repo demand falls, and the structural vulnerability shrinks on its own. The trade does not need a crisis to get smaller. It just needs the profit to disappear.

That process has been underway for most of 2026. The question the data does not yet answer is whether the remaining $900 billion can exit as quietly as the $360 billion that already left.

The Number That Cannot Be Unloaded Overnight

The specific problem is not leverage in the abstract. It is the combination of zero-haircut repo funding, day-to-day financing, and the absence of any natural secondary buyer when positions are forced out simultaneously.

When a large equity fund sells stocks under stress, thousands of other equity investors can absorb the supply at a discount. When a basis fund sells Treasury bonds into a dislocated market, primary dealers are the absorbers of last resort. In March 2020, primary dealers could not perform that function at scale. The Fed stepped in because no one else could.

The $900 billion in basis positions outstanding today requires the same infrastructure to unwind as the roughly $400 billion in 2020. Primary dealer capacity has not expanded proportionally. The position has grown. The shock absorber hasn’t.

Plain English

Even though United States government bonds are considered the safest investment on earth, a large group of hedge funds quietly borrow nearly $2 trillion overnight to run a trade that earns tiny fractions of a percent, and that borrowing has to be renewed every single morning. I argue this because when that overnight funding dries up — when the banks providing it decide to cut back during a market shock — those hedge funds are forced to sell their Treasury bonds all at once, flooding the market with supply at exactly the wrong time. Although the trade has gotten somewhat smaller in recent months, it is still roughly double the size it reached just before the Federal Reserve had to inject $1.7 trillion to prevent a Treasury market breakdown in March 2020. If the same unwind happens again, it pushes up mortgage rates, car loan rates, and every other borrowing cost across the economy almost immediately — and you may find your own monthly payments rising sharply before anyone in the mainstream press explains why.
