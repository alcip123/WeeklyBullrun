149 Nuclear Verdicts Last Year — Casualty Insurers Are Still Reserving Like It’s 2019

The P&C industry’s strong results in 2026 rest on workers’ compensation and property reserve releases, not on casualty lines — and casualty is exactly where a decade of accelerating jury awards has built a gap that actuarial models were not built to measure.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  October 9, 2026  ·  9:37 AM ET

The Surplus That Came From Workers’ Comp

P&C insurers are reporting some of the cleanest combined ratios in years. Travelers posted an 84.7 in Q2 2026. Chubb came in at 83.8. Both look like precision underwriting at work.

They aren’t. They are the product of prior-year reserve releases from the wrong lines.

Travelers’ Q2 favorable development totaled $578 million, more than covering the $518 million in catastrophe losses the quarter produced. The company attributed those releases to better-than-expected loss experience in workers’ compensation over multiple accident years and in commercial property for recent years. Within business insurance, analysts estimate more than $200 million came from workers’ comp and roughly $80 million from commercial property.

General liability, commercial auto, umbrella, and excess casualty contributed almost nothing to those releases. The underlying combined ratio — which strips out catastrophe losses and prior-year development to isolate current accident year results — rose 0.5 percentage points year over year even as the headline improved by nearly 14 points.

The improvement is real. The source of it isn’t.

How Outside Money Changed What a Verdict Is Worth

The shift in verdict sizes tracks a structural change in how large civil cases are financed.

Third-party litigation funding — outside investors providing capital to plaintiffs in exchange for a share of the award — has grown to $16.1 billion in assets under management in the U.S., more than doubling from under $10 billion five years ago. New capital commitments reached $2.8 billion in 2025, a 23% increase from 2024. The model is straightforward: a funder backs a plaintiff, absorbs litigation costs, and collects 20% to 35% of any settlement or verdict. Their incentive is not a fair outcome. It is the largest outcome.

When funders enter a case, early settlement offers disappear. Cases go to trial. Juries hear arguments constructed by well-capitalized legal teams with expert witnesses and years of discovery. The results show in the data.

Nuclear verdicts — jury awards exceeding $10 million — numbered 135 in 2024, a 52% increase from 2023. In 2025, there were 149 such verdicts totaling $25.1 billion. RPS data shows median nuclear verdict size increased more than 25% over the past decade. Less than 15% of the average award ties to economic loss. The rest is pain and suffering, punitive damages, and jury-amplified anger. The Swiss Re Social Inflation Index, which measures the gap between legal cost inflation and general economic inflation, peaked at 7% in 2023, the highest level in two decades.

Insurance actuaries set reserves using historical loss development patterns. The patterns changed. The models did not.

Thirty-Five Billion Dollars of Adverse Development Already on the Books

In September 2026, Moody’s published a review counting cumulative adverse reserve development exceeding $35 billion in general liability over the previous eight years, approaching $100 billion once commercial auto is included. The rating agency attributed the pattern directly to social inflation, litigation funding, and nuclear verdicts.

Milliman’s data show that total adverse prior-year development across all liability lines reached $15.8 billion in 2024 alone — the highest on record for those segments and more than double the $3.7 billion recorded in 2023. Assured Research estimated the “other liability occurrence” line — general liability, umbrella, and excess claims on occurrence-form policies — was $12.5 billion deficient as of year-end 2025. Of that, $10.5 billion is concentrated in accident years 2021 through 2024.

Those accident years are still open. Casualty claims develop over years. A verdict entered today may apply to an incident from 2022, on a policy reserved in 2023 using loss development factors calibrated before nuclear verdicts started compounding. The gap between what was reserved and what will ultimately be paid is not a projection. It is already appearing in development tables.

$15.8 billion in adverse development across liability lines in a single calendar year is not noise. It is a signal.

The Carriers Running the Shortest Clock

Chubb’s CEO Evan Greenberg said on the Q2 2026 earnings call that U.S. casualty loss costs are rising 6% to 7% annually for primary casualty lines and 9.5% to 12% for excess. He added: “There is zero evidence across the industry that loss costs have abated.”

Chubb writes substantial excess casualty — the layer that pays when verdicts exceed $25 million, $50 million, or $100 million. As nuclear verdict sizes have grown, those excess layers erode faster than original reserve assumptions allowed. A verdict that settles at $22 million costs Chubb nothing. The same case settled at $35 million costs Chubb $10 million on a policy with a $25 million attachment point.

Travelers carries the largest commercial auto book among publicly traded P&C insurers. Commercial auto is the single most nuclear-verdict-exposed product line in the industry. A truck driver at fault in an accident with serious injuries is a $30 million exposure in ways it was not in 2017. The company’s favorable development has been coming from workers’ comp, a line that benefits from decades of state-level reform and favorable medical cost trends. That runway is not unlimited.

Lockton data from Q2 2026 shows lead umbrella pricing rising 6.2% and excess casualty rising 8.2%. Chubb’s stated loss cost trend for those lines is 9.5% to 12%. When pricing runs persistently below loss cost trends, reserves get thinner every quarter the policy remains in force.

The Line Item That Will Move First

The “other liability occurrence” column in the prior-year development tables of a quarterly 10-Q filing is the place to watch. This line captures general liability, umbrella, and excess claims on occurrence-form policies. It stays open for years. It does not close with a payment. It closes with a verdict or settlement that may arrive five years after the underlying incident.

Three consecutive quarters of adverse development in this column would indicate that actuarial patterns are breaking. Chubb and Everest Re showed diverging casualty reserve trends relative to Travelers and CNA in Q2 2026 data reviewed by actuarial analysts. That divergence is the early warning.

When adverse development appears, it flows directly to operating income. A $500 million reserve addition at a carrier writing $5 billion in annual earned premium produces a 10-point combined ratio hit in the quarter it is recognized.

Favorable development from workers’ comp and property has been absorbing those hits before they reach the headline numbers. Workers’ comp claims are now beginning to trend upward as pandemic-era medical deferrals mature. When the workers’ comp tailwind normalizes, the absorption capacity shrinks. The casualty additions do not shrink with it.

What Would Have to Be True for This to Not Happen

Federal tort reform is the most direct solution. Disclosure requirements on litigation funding would reveal funder involvement to defendants and allow earlier settlements before cases reach trial. The current administration has pushed for legislation. Nothing has passed.

Rate increases reduce the problem on new business but do not close the gap on policies already written. Pricing the 2026 accident year at accurate loss cost trends does not improve the reserve adequacy of the 2021 accident year. That gap is already locked in.

One genuine path to a softer outcome: if nuclear verdict counts plateau or decline in 2026 and 2027, actuaries could extend favorable development assumptions and delay recognition. The 2025 count of 149 nuclear verdicts was up from 135 in 2024. There is no current evidence of a reversal.

The casualty reserve correction is not a question of whether. It is a question of when and which quarter it first appears in which carrier’s filing.

The favorable development will run out before the adverse development does.

Plain English

Even though the biggest property and casualty insurance companies in the country are reporting some of their best results in years, the profits are mostly coming from one specific type of insurance — workers’ compensation — that has been running unusually well, not from the liability coverage that protects businesses when someone sues them. I argue this because jury awards in civil lawsuits have roughly doubled in median size over the past decade, and a growing industry of outside investors is funding plaintiffs to take cases to trial rather than settle cheaply, producing far larger verdicts than insurance companies accounted for when they set aside money for future claims. Although the companies still look profitable for now, the gap between what they reserved and what they will eventually pay is already showing up in industry data, estimated at over $12 billion in one category of coverage alone. When the workers’ compensation profits that are currently masking the problem normalize, you may start seeing sudden, large earnings misses from major insurance companies — not because something new went wrong, but because the liability was always there.
