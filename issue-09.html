The Capacity Tax — How America’s Power Grid Shortage Is Making Merchant Generators Rich and Leaving Load-Serving Utilities Exposed

PJM’s capacity auction has cleared at its price cap for two straight delivery years, producing a direct windfall for merchant generators while load-serving utilities absorb billions in procurement costs they cannot recover until state commissions approve rate cases more than a year later.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  September 15, 2026  ·  9:20 AM ET

Two Years at the Price Cap, and the Reserve Shortfall Is Growing

The United States added roughly 50 gigawatts of new electricity generation capacity in 2025, more than in any year since post-WWII utility construction. Wind and solar installations are running at record pace. PJM Interconnection, the grid operator serving 13 states and 65 million people, failed to procure its required backup power reserve for the second consecutive delivery year.

Capacity and energy are separate products. Energy is the electricity produced when a generator runs. Capacity is the guarantee that a generator can run on demand, regardless of weather or grid conditions. A solar farm earns energy revenue when the sun shines; it earns a fraction of full capacity credit because it may not be producing during a July heat wave peak. A gas turbine that can start in 10 minutes earns full capacity credit. As coal plants and aging gas units retire, the grid loses dispatchable, always-available generation even as total nameplate capacity grows.

PJM's 2026-2027 Base Residual Auction, cleared in July 2025, set capacity prices at $329.17 per megawatt-day. That is the market's established price cap, hit for the second year in a row. The 2025-2026 delivery year cleared at $269.92 per megawatt-day. In the 2024-2025 auction, the price was $28.92. The 11-fold increase over three delivery years represents the market's signal that PJM is structurally short of reliable generation.

PJM fell 6.8 gigawatts short of its 20 percent installed reserve margin target in its most recent auction. That shortfall increased from 6.5 gigawatts the year before. The grid is not closing the gap.

How a Data Center in Northern Virginia Becomes a Rate Increase in Ohio

The supply problem has a specific cause. PJM's 2026 Long-Term Load Forecast projects 32 gigawatts of peak demand growth from 2024 through 2030. Data centers account for 94 percent of that growth. Northern Virginia, home to the world's largest concentration of data center infrastructure, absorbs roughly 25 to 30 percent of all PJM load. A demand surge in one corner of the Mid-Atlantic has become a systemic constraint across 13 states.

PJM's capacity auctions are conducted three years before the delivery year. When the 2025-2026 auction cleared at $269.92 per megawatt-day in July 2024, independent power producers locked in that price through hedges and forward contracts. Vistra Energy, NRG Energy, and Constellation Energy built their 2025 and 2026 earnings guidance around those numbers. The price was set; the question was only when the revenue would flow.

PJM estimated in 2025 that data centers accounted for 63 percent of the capacity price increase in the 2025-2026 auction, translating to $9.3 billion in annual customer costs embedded in higher electric rates across the footprint. That $9.3 billion flows from ratepayers to whoever holds the generation. Load-serving utilities in the middle collect nothing. They pass through what they procure.

The Load-Serving Utilities Absorbing the Procurement Gap

The earnings divergence between generators and distributors is now measurable. Vistra Corp reported adjusted EBITDA of $3.261 billion in the first half of 2026, up 26 percent from $2.589 billion in the same period of 2025. The company's full-year 2026 EBITDA guidance stands at $6.8 to $7.6 billion, with management explicitly citing higher PJM capacity revenues as the primary driver. Constellation Energy, which combined with Calpine in early 2025 to form the largest generation fleet in the country, raised full-year 2026 adjusted operating earnings guidance to $11.50 to $12.50 per share. Its capacity reference prices in the Eastern Mid-Atlantic region reached $289.67 per megawatt-day in Q2 2026, up from $125.71 per megawatt-day in Q2 2025.

Load-serving utilities own distribution wires and serve retail customers. They do not own significant dispatchable generation in PJM. They are net buyers of capacity at whatever the auction clears. FirstEnergy's Ohio customers saw combined energy and capacity rates rise 14 percent to 10.14 cents per kilowatt-hour in June 2026. AES Ohio raised rates 15 percent in the same month. Those increases reached customers months after the underlying procurement costs began hitting the utilities' books.

The recovery mechanism is a state utility commission rate case. A rate case in Ohio, New Jersey, or Pennsylvania runs 12 to 24 months from filing to final order. FirstEnergy filed a three-year rate case with Ohio regulators in May 2026, seeking $800 million in annual new revenue. That case will not resolve before mid-2027 at the earliest. The capacity costs incurred during the 2026-2027 delivery year sit on FirstEnergy's income statement while the case is pending. The lag between cost and recovery is an earnings drag that does not appear in the capacity auction data and does not generate headlines during the IPP earnings calls.

When the Hyperscaler Demand Forecast Comes In at 60 Percent

The entire capacity crisis is built on a load forecast. The forecast has already been revised once.

Exelon CEO Calvin Butler noted in Q1 2026 earnings that there is a "high probability" data center load growth in New Jersey falls 40 percent short of current projections. PJM revised its own load forecast downward in early 2026 after implementing stricter vetting standards for data center interconnection requests. An interconnection request is not a built data center. The pipeline of announced projects in PJM has consistently included capacity that never materializes on the projected timeline.

Supply is responding to the price signal. Vistra is investing $4.5 to $5 billion in capacity additions in 2026-2027, including its $4 billion Cogentrix acquisition adding 5.5 gigawatts of natural gas generation. NRG Energy agreed in 2025 to acquire 13 gigawatts of gas-fired capacity from LS Power for $12 billion. Constellation cleared 18,875 megawatts in the most recent PJM auction for 2028-2029 delivery. The $329-per-megawatt-day price signal is the highest investment incentive the U.S. power market has sent in at least two decades. Capital is responding at scale.

Capacity prices for future delivery years are set by future auctions. The 2027-2028 delivery year cleared at $333.44 per megawatt-day. The 2028-2029 auction clears in December 2026. If supply additions arrive faster than data center load materializes, the clearing price falls below the cap. Every earnings model built on sustained $300-plus capacity revenues reprices on that result.

The December Auction and the Backstop Filing That Could Reshape the Market

Two events in the next three months will determine whether the current structure holds.

The first is the December 2026 Base Residual Auction for the 2028-2029 delivery year. The established price cap is $120,147 per megawatt-year, equal to $329 per megawatt-day. Three consecutive auctions at the cap would indicate that announced capacity additions have not yet closed the reserve margin gap. A clearing price materially below the cap would indicate the supply response is working faster than the demand build.

The second is PJM's Reliability Backstop Procurement, filed with the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission on July 31, 2026. PJM is seeking authority to procure up to 6,800 megawatts of capacity outside the standard competitive auction at negotiated terms. If FERC approves, some portion of capacity procurement bypasses the market price signal. Generators oppose the mechanism because it dilutes their capacity revenues. Load-serving utilities are divided. FirstEnergy CEO Brian Tierney has called the auction results "the canary in the coal mine" while also opposing parts of the backstop design as insufficient.

A longer-run political risk is reregulation. Executives at Exelon, FirstEnergy, PPL, and American Electric Power have discussed state-level reregulation that would allow them to own and build generation as a rate-regulated monopoly asset. Active lobbying is underway in Ohio, New Jersey, and Illinois. Reregulation would transfer capacity revenue from independent generators to regulated utilities and compress the IPP earnings multiple structurally, not cyclically.

If New Gas Capacity Closes the Gap Before Data Centers Fill the Grid

The IPP earnings thesis fails if the announced capacity investments -- Cogentrix, NRG's LS Power acquisition, other new gas projects in the PJM queue -- reduce the reserve margin shortfall before data center demand materializes at the projected rate. The current shortfall is 6.8 gigawatts. The announced new capacity exceeds that figure if interconnection approvals arrive on schedule, which they historically do not. But the price signal is strong enough that some portion of the investment will arrive faster than past cycles suggest.

The regulated utility thesis fails if state commissions allow interim cost recovery mechanisms that reduce the rate case lag. Several PJM states are under pressure from rate increases of 14 to 15 percent that have reached retail customers ahead of any formal rate case resolution. If regulators grant interim recovery, the deferred earnings drag on FirstEnergy, AES Ohio, and PPL closes faster than the 18-month average implies.

The Number That Grows Every Year

The 2024-2025 delivery year cleared at $28.92 per megawatt-day. The 2027-2028 delivery year cleared at $333.44. That movement happened in three auctions. PJM's reserve margin shortfall grew from zero to 6.5 gigawatts, then to 6.8 gigawatts, in two years.

Coal plant retirements do not reverse. The dispatchable capacity leaving the grid is not coming back.

Plain English

Even though electric companies have been building record amounts of solar panels and wind turbines, the part of the power system that guarantees your electricity stays on during the hottest day of summer or the coldest week of winter is actually getting tighter, not looser. I argue this because wind and solar farms can only generate power when the weather cooperates, and the older gas and coal plants that could run on command are retiring faster than they are being replaced, while thousands of data centers in Virginia are consuming a bigger share of the grid every year. Although your electric bill may not have fully reflected this yet, the utilities responsible for keeping the power on have been paying record prices to secure backup electricity since mid-2025, and state regulators are only beginning to approve the rate increases needed to cover those costs. Over the next year or two, you may start seeing your electric bill rise by 10 to 15 percent, and not because you are using more electricity, but because the insurance policy that keeps your power from going out has become genuinely more expensive.
