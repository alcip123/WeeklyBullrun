The AI Power Tax — How Data Centers Are Repricing America’s Industrial Heartland

PJM’s capacity auction has set a record for three consecutive years. The price cap that contained it has just expired.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  October 20, 2026  ·  9:13 AM ET

From $2.2 Billion to $14.7 Billion in a Single Auction

The most consequential power auction in the United States happens once a year, takes about a day to run, and receives almost no coverage in financial media. Almost none of the equity analysts covering America’s industrial manufacturers have modeled what it does to their clients’ costs.

That changed in July 2024.

PJM Interconnection, the grid operator serving 65 million people across 13 states and Washington D.C., runs an annual capacity auction where electricity generators bid to be available during peak demand. For most of the past decade, the clearing price hovered below $50 per megawatt-day. The total annual cost to electricity consumers across PJM ran around $2.2 billion. Then the July 2024 auction settled at $269.92 per megawatt-day, a ninefold jump from the prior year’s $28.92. The total annual cost to customers climbed from $2.2 billion to $14.7 billion.

In one auction, $12.5 billion in electricity costs transferred from consumers to generators. PJM’s independent market monitor, citing the scale of new data center load, described the auction as not competitive. The phrase did not mean the auction was rigged. It meant demand so decisively exceeded supply that the market was functioning as rationing by price rather than price discovery.

The Capacity Fee Nobody in Finance Is Tracking

The mechanics of PJM’s capacity market are worth understanding precisely because they are so rarely explained.

Every year, PJM conducts a Base Residual Auction roughly three years ahead of a delivery period. Generators offer the minimum price at which they are willing to be available to produce power during peak demand hours. PJM sets a demand curve based on projected load. When projected demand exceeds available capacity offers, prices rise. Every electricity consumer in PJM pays the clearing price as a capacity charge layered on their bill, separate from the actual energy they use. The charge reflects not the power they consumed but the insurance cost of having enough generation standing by.

For decades, the auction operated quietly. Generation additions kept pace with modest demand growth. Then two things happened simultaneously: approximately 6,600 megawatts of coal and gas capacity retired before the July 2024 auction as aging plants became uneconomical, and data centers began connecting to PJM at a pace grid planners had never modeled. PJM’s long-term forecast now projects summer peak demand growing from 160 gigawatts in 2025 to 253 gigawatts by 2046, a 58 percent increase driven primarily by data center expansion. That demand growth is arriving into a grid that cannot add new generation fast enough to absorb it.

The Ohio Brick Maker Paying Eight Times What It Did Two Years Ago

The capacity price jump is not abstract. It appears directly on industrial electricity bills as a specific line item.

One Ohio brick manufacturer reported that its monthly capacity charge rose from $1,600 to $12,000 after the new pricing took effect. A company operating multiple plants across Pennsylvania and Ohio saw its combined annual capacity charges jump from $200,000 to $1.2 million. These are not companies that ordered more power. They are companies that happen to share a grid with the data centers. Industrial electricity prices in Pennsylvania rose 31 percent year over year as of December 2025. In Ohio, they rose 26 percent. The national average for industrial users over the same period rose 7 percent.

The companies absorbing this cost are concentrated in energy-intensive sectors: steel, aluminum, glass, chemicals, and building materials. They compete in markets where margins are thin and input costs determine survival. A $1 million annual increase in capacity charges for a plant running 10 percent operating margins requires $10 million in additional revenue just to hold earnings flat. Most equity models for these companies were built around electricity prices that no longer exist.

What Happens When the Price Cap Disappears

The 2026/2027 capacity auction cleared at $329.17 per megawatt-day, another record. Without a price cap negotiated with Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro, PJM’s own modeling projected the price would have settled at roughly $389 per megawatt-day. The 2027/2028 auction then set yet another record at $333.44 per megawatt-day. Three consecutive record-setting auctions. Three consecutive caps.

The cap has now expired for the 2028/2029 delivery year.

The independent market monitor’s characterization of the 2027/2028 auction was identical to its description of the prior two: not competitive. Data center demand so exceeded available capacity that the auction was not functioning as intended. A similar demand imbalance in an uncapped environment would clear above $389. The precise level depends on retirements, new generation additions, and data center growth between now and the next auction in July 2027. None of those three variables is moving in the direction of relief.

Three Signals in the Auction Coming Next July

Three data points will determine whether the next auction extends this pattern or breaks from it.

First, whether FERC reinstates a price cap before July 2027. The independent market monitor has flagged competitive concerns in three consecutive auctions. Federal regulators have tools to impose interim price protection, and several legislators representing industrial states within PJM have indicated interest in capacity market reform. A formal FERC proceeding before the next auction would change the calculus entirely.

Second, the pace at which new generation reaches PJM’s interconnection queue. Approximately 57 gigawatts of new supply projects currently have active interconnection agreements. Getting from agreement to operational status takes years in most cases, but even partial additions arriving before July 2027 shift the supply curve meaningfully.

Third, whether major hyperscalers follow the model Talen Energy and Microsoft established at Three Mile Island. Talen’s agreement to restart a 1.1-gigawatt nuclear unit and sell its output directly to Microsoft removes that load from the capacity auction entirely. If Amazon, Google, and Meta pursue similar dedicated-generation arrangements at scale, data center demand in the auction could plateau before the cap disappears.

If Steel Plants and Data Centers Stop Competing for the Same Electrons

The thesis breaks if new generation capacity arrives faster than data center demand grows.

The clearest path to that outcome is direct contracting between hyperscalers and generators. Microsoft, Google, and Amazon have each signed or announced long-term power purchase agreements for new nuclear and gas capacity in the past 18 months. If these agreements consistently remove load from PJM capacity auctions rather than adding to them, the demand curve that drove the $28.92-to-$333 climb moderates before it continues. The 57-gigawatt interconnection pipeline could also bring meaningful new supply online faster than historical timelines suggest, particularly if regulatory streamlining accelerates approvals.

The thesis also breaks if Congress acts. A statutory price cap or mandatory reserve-margin requirement that forces faster generation additions would rebalance the auction. The political coalition is visible: manufacturing-state representatives in both parties have incentives to act. The legislative timeline is not.

What a $2.2 Billion Constellation Revenue Line Actually Means

Constellation Energy cleared 18,025 megawatts in the 2027/2028 capacity auction, generating approximately $2.2 billion in annual capacity revenues from that single result. Talen Energy cleared 8,745 megawatts for approximately $1.1 billion. Vistra Energy cleared 10,566 megawatts for approximately $1.3 billion. On the day PJM announced the 2026/2027 results, Talen shares rose 9 percent, Constellation 5 percent, and NRG 6 percent.

That $4.6 billion in combined capacity revenues for three generators is a direct transfer from the businesses paying capacity charges across PJM’s 13-state footprint. Every capacity dollar a generator earns is a capacity dollar an electricity consumer pays. The Ohio brick maker at $12,000 a month and the Pennsylvania chemicals plant at $1.2 million a year are the other side of Constellation’s earnings beat.

The capacity market is obscure enough to escape equity research and large enough to move sector margins. When the price cap expires, the bound disappears with it. The companies paying attention are buying generation assets. The companies that aren’t are running factories on the same grid.

Plain English

Even though the technology companies building massive data centers are creating jobs and investment across the country, they are also quietly raising the electricity bills of factories, warehouses, and small manufacturers in 13 states stretching from New Jersey to Illinois. I argue this because the grid that powers that region holds an annual auction where power plants bid to supply electricity during hot summers and cold winters, and the arrival of enormous server farms needing uninterrupted power has sent prices in that auction up more than 1,000 percent over just a few years. Although the federal government placed a cap on how high those prices could rise for the past three auctions, that cap has now expired, and the next auction will be uncapped. If you own or work at a business in Pennsylvania, Ohio, New Jersey, or nearby states, your electricity bill may keep rising not because you are using more power but because the companies building the infrastructure behind artificial intelligence need the same electrons your factory does.
