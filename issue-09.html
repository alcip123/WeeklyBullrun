The Seven-Year Wait — AI Is Spending $650 Billion on Data Centers That Can’t Get Power

Hyperscalers have committed record capital to computing infrastructure, but the grid connection process takes five to seven years and the construction timeline runs one to three. The gap between those numbers is where the trade lives.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  September 29, 2026  ·  9:28 AM ET

Why $650 Billion in Announced Capex Won’t All Translate Into Compute

Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Microsoft are spending more than $650 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026. Most of the buildings will go up. Not all of them will get power on time.

Up to half of the data center capacity planned for 2026 is projected to be delayed, according to analysis from Network World and Latitude Media. Seven gigawatts of AI capacity is currently in acute crisis from grid connection delays. The buildings exist or are under construction. The electricity is still in a government queue.

The binding constraint on the AI infrastructure buildout has shifted. It is no longer chips or capital. It is the process of connecting a new large power consumer to the electrical grid, a process that takes between five and seven years in most U.S. markets, against a construction cycle of one to three years. The mismatch is not a temporary administrative problem. It is structural.

The Queue: 2,061 Gigawatts Waiting in a Process Designed for a Different Era

Every new large power consumer that wants to connect to the U.S. transmission grid must first apply to its regional grid operator for an interconnection study. The study determines what infrastructure upgrades the existing grid needs to accommodate the new connection, and who pays for them.

The system was designed for a world where major new grid connections were occasional events. It now faces the largest single surge in industrial electricity demand in American history.

As of the end of 2025, 2,061 gigawatts of generation and storage capacity were actively seeking grid interconnection, according to the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s annual Queued Up report. For context, total installed U.S. electrical capacity is approximately 1,400 gigawatts. The queue is nearly 50 percent larger than the entire existing grid.

The historical success rate is worse than the headline numbers suggest. Of all capacity that submitted interconnection applications between 2000 and 2020, only 13 percent was actually built by the end of 2025. Seventy-five percent withdrew. In 2025 alone, the ratio reached 14.2 to one: for every gigawatt that came online, 14 gigawatts gave up and withdrew from the process. The queue is not a pipeline. It is a filter that rejects the vast majority of what enters it.

Wait times reflect the pressure. The median project completed in 2025 spent over five years in the interconnection process. In PJM, the grid operator covering Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Maryland, and the Northern Virginia data center corridor, AI campus filings now face waits approaching seven years. A standard hyperscale data center lease runs five. The math does not work.

The Data Center Landlords Who Signed Leases on Land Without Power

Data center real estate investment trusts and private developers have committed tens of billions in capital to sites and construction in markets where grid connections are years away. The business model requires delivering an energized facility to a tenant within 12 to 18 months of lease signing. A seven-year queue breaks that model.

The market response is visible in how hyperscalers are routing around the problem. Behind-the-meter power solutions, where a company contracts for generation directly at or near its facility rather than drawing from the transmission grid, are rising from 10 to 20 percent of incremental data center power demand in 2025 to a projected 50 to 60 percent by 2030, according to industry analysis.

Nuclear is the preferred workaround. Microsoft committed to the entire 20-year output of the restarted Three Mile Island plant in Pennsylvania, now operating as the Crane Clean Energy Center, with a FERC transmission waiver granted in June 2026 accelerating delivery to the second half of 2027. Amazon secured a 1.9 gigawatt power purchase agreement with Talen Energy’s Susquehanna nuclear plant in Pennsylvania, running through 2042. Both deals bypass the interconnection queue entirely: the plants already have grid connections. The hyperscaler buys the electrons at the source.

The workaround is limited. There are not many dormant nuclear plants available for new purchase agreements. Amazon and Microsoft have already absorbed the most accessible ones. Data center operators without nuclear partnerships are still in the queue.

The Grid Contractors With a $48 Billion Backlog and More Coming

The constrained market does not punish everyone equally. The companies that build and upgrade transmission infrastructure are running at full capacity and extending their lead.

Quanta Services, the largest U.S. electrical infrastructure contractor, reported first-quarter 2026 revenue of $7.87 billion, up 26.3 percent from the same period a year earlier, with total backlog hitting a record $48.47 billion. Management identified a $2.4 trillion addressable market through 2030 across utility upgrades, new generation connections, and large-load customer projects. The stock is up more than 66 percent year to date. Grid equipment manufacturers including Eaton and GE Vernova are tracking similarly.

U.S. electric utility capital expenditures are projected to reach nearly $1.3 trillion between 2026 and 2030, according to S&P Global Market Intelligence. That spending flows to contractors and equipment makers before it flows through to utility shareholders, which is why grid infrastructure funds have outperformed the regulated utility sector by more than 30 percentage points in 2026. The market already knows where the money is going. It has not fully priced how long the bottleneck lasts.

What FERC’s June 18 Orders Can and Cannot Fix

On June 18, 2026, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission issued orders under Section 206 of the Federal Power Act to every U.S. regional grid operator, giving each 60 days to produce accelerated interconnection programs for large industrial loads, including AI data center operators. It was the most direct federal intervention in the interconnection process in years.

The orders acknowledge the severity of the problem. Whether they solve it depends on which part of the problem is actually binding.

The interconnection process has two distinct bottlenecks. The first is administrative: the backlog of studies, the documentation cycles, the review queues inside each grid operator’s office. FERC can attack that directly, and the June orders are aimed there. The second is physical: connecting a new large load to the grid requires new transmission lines, substation upgrades, and high-voltage transformer installations. Transformer lead times from major suppliers currently run 18 to 24 months. FERC has no jurisdiction over that supply chain.

Regulatory reforms can reduce a seven-year wait. They cannot reduce it to one.

If the Hyperscalers Build Their Own Grid

The thesis breaks under two conditions.

The first is behind-the-meter scaling ahead of schedule. If the shift from 20 percent to 60 percent of data center power coming from on-site sources happens by 2028 rather than 2030, the grid queue constraint shrinks faster than the infrastructure investment cycle assumes. Small modular reactors, on-site gas turbines, and large-scale battery storage all reduce the queue dependency if they deploy at commercial scale. Google’s acquisition of the energy company Intersect Power in 2026 is an early signal that hyperscalers are building parallel private energy systems rather than waiting for the public grid.

The second is a demand correction. Hyperscalers have cut capex before. A meaningful miss in AI revenue growth would translate into billions of dollars of canceled data center projects within quarters, not years. Quanta Services’ $48 billion backlog is contracted, but the pipeline behind it depends on the demand continuing. If it does not, the contractor trade weakens before the queue ever clears.

Neither scenario is imminent. But both are risks that infrastructure investors are tracking.

The Backlog Grows Every Year Regardless

The interconnection queue has expanded in almost every year since 2010. Process reforms, FERC orders, and ISO pilot programs have generated headlines without bending the curve. The 2,061 gigawatts waiting at the end of 2025 was down from a peak of nearly 2,600 gigawatts in 2023, but only because a record volume of projects withdrew. The queue shrank because applicants gave up. Not because the grid got faster.

That dynamic is not changing in 2026. The equity market implications are already visible in contractor backlogs and grid fund performance. What is less visible is the quiet drag on the hyperscaler revenue story, as planned compute capacity slips by months and years. Announced capex is not the same as deployed capacity. The grid knows the difference.

Plain English

Even though major technology companies keep announcing they will spend hundreds of billions of dollars building enormous computer facilities for artificial intelligence, many of those buildings are going to be delayed before they can even turn on a computer. I argue this because getting a new building connected to the electrical grid in the United States now requires going through a review process that typically takes five to seven years to complete, while construction itself only takes one to three, and the system was never designed to handle the number of companies all applying at once. Although some companies have found workarounds, like striking deals to buy the output of entire nuclear power plants that already have grid connections, those options are limited and the largest ones are already taken. What this means for ordinary people is that the AI services they use may improve more slowly than the spending announcements suggest, and electricity bills are likely to keep rising as utilities spend more than a trillion dollars building out a grid that should have been expanded years ago.
