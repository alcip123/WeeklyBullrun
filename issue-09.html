The Safety-Net Squeeze — Two Medicaid Policy Shifts Are Converging on Hospitals That Cannot Turn Patients Away

The federal government cut disproportionate share hospital payments by more than half while simultaneously creating work requirements that will remove 6 million people from Medicaid by December, and both policies hit the same hospitals hardest.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  September 4, 2026  ·  9:30 AM ET

Why the Hospitals Treating the Most Uninsured Are Running Out of Federal Offset

America's emergency rooms are legally required to treat every patient who arrives, insured or not. The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act has enforced that obligation since 1986. It does not distinguish between a patient with insurance and a patient without one. Treatment must happen regardless of ability to pay.

The federal mechanism that partially compensates hospitals for meeting that obligation has just been cut by more than half. At the same time, Medicaid work requirements introduced by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act are reclassifying millions of people from covered patients into uninsured ones. The government has removed both the payor status and the offset simultaneously, at the same hospitals that are least able to absorb the loss.

Hospital stocks have not moved in a way that reflects this. The compounding revenue pressure is time-lagged: the DSH cut began October 1, 2025, and the Medicaid coverage losses are accelerating through state systems throughout 2026. Both effects are measurable in current financial statements. The math does not work.

The Cut That Congress Delayed 13 Times and Finally Let Through

The Medicaid Disproportionate Share Hospital program compensates hospitals that treat high proportions of Medicaid and uninsured patients. It exists because Medicaid reimburses hospitals at rates below the cost of care, and DSH payments bridge that gap. The program has historically supported more than 2,500 hospitals across the country.

Congress voted to cut those payments 13 consecutive times between 2012 and 2025. Each time, the cut was delayed. The reasoning was consistent: hospitals serving the poorest patients operate on the thinnest margins, and removing their federal support would accelerate closures, particularly in rural areas and urban cores where no alternative existed.

The 14th action did not produce a delay. The DSH cut took effect October 1, 2025. The reduction is $8 billion per year, representing more than 50 percent of the entire DSH program. The three-year impact totals $24 billion.

Before the cut, the average operating margin for the nonprofit hospital sector ran approximately 1.5 percent. The Medicaid and CHIP Payment and Access Commission calculated that DSH payments represent 3.6 percent of operating revenue for hospitals that receive them. A cut that removes that buffer entirely is not a marginal adjustment at a 1.5-percent-margin institution. The cut removes the margin.

Six Million Medicaid Patients Converting to Uninsured by December

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, requires able-bodied Medicaid enrollees in the 44 states that accepted the ACA Medicaid expansion to complete 80 hours per month of work, community service, or educational enrollment to maintain coverage. Nebraska became the first state to implement requirements under this law in May 2026. Full rollout across all 44 states and Washington, D.C. is scheduled by December.

The Kaiser Family Foundation projects that work requirements will remove 6 million people from Medicaid rolls by December 2026. People losing Medicaid do not stop needing healthcare. They stop arriving as Medicaid patients and start arriving as uninsured patients.

The Commonwealth Fund calculated the operating margin effect of this transition: work requirements in expansion states reduce operating margins at safety-net hospitals by an average of 29.6 percent. That calculation does not include the concurrent DSH payment cut. A hospital absorbing a 3.6-point margin loss from DSH reductions while simultaneously absorbing a 29.6-point reduction from payer mix deterioration is not managing two headwinds. It is managing a collapse.

Premier Inc., a healthcare analytics company, estimates total hospital revenue losses across 2026 and 2027 will reach $68.6 billion from these combined Medicaid policy changes.

How Uncompensated Care Moves from Safety-Net Hospitals to Publicly Traded Chains

Safety-net hospitals serve specific geography. They concentrate in urban cores and rural counties where Medicaid and uninsured populations are densest. When a safety-net hospital reduces services or closes a unit, demand does not disappear. It moves to the next nearest facility, often a for-profit or larger nonprofit system.

HCA Healthcare reported $15 billion in total uncompensated care for the second quarter of 2026 alone, at an estimated cost to the company of $1.445 billion. For the six months ended June 30, the estimated cost of uncompensated care was $2.697 billion. Those figures reflect the environment before 6 million people have completed their transition out of Medicaid coverage. They will grow as the transition accelerates through the second half of the year.

Tenet Healthcare reported that Medicaid represented 13.8 percent of net patient service revenues for the first half of 2026. Tenet also recognized $92 million in favorable out-of-period Medicaid supplemental revenues in the second quarter, a figure tied to the supplemental payment structure that the DSH cut reduced. As state Medicaid supplemental programs adjust to the new federal ceiling, that revenue line will not repeat at the same level.

The mechanism is not subtle. Fewer covered patients means more uncompensated care. More uncompensated care means a larger cost that is shifted onto private payers through higher charges. Higher charges for private insurers means higher premiums for anyone with employer-sponsored health coverage.

The REIT Flagging a Quarter of Its Revenue in the Footnotes

Hospital REITs hold the real estate that healthcare operators lease. The financial health of the REIT depends directly on the financial health of its tenants. When Medicaid revenues decline for operators, their ability to pay rent declines with them.

Omega Healthcare Investors is the largest publicly traded REIT focused on skilled nursing facilities, which derive approximately 60 percent of revenues from Medicaid. In its most recent 10-Q, Omega disclosed that 22 operators were on a cash basis of rental revenue recognition as of June 30, 2026, representing 22.2 percent of Omega's total revenues. Cash basis accounting is applied when the financial condition of a tenant makes normal accrual recognition unreliable: revenue is recorded only when cash is actually received, because the tenant's viability is in question.

A portfolio where more than one in five revenue dollars is from financially stressed tenants is not a portfolio trading at a premium multiple. The portfolio's mean EBITDAR coverage ratio was 1.65 times as of the most recent disclosure. The 22 stressed operators disclosed on cash basis had trailing 12-month coverage below 0.87 times, meaning they are generating insufficient cash flow to cover rent and remain financially viable without restructuring or additional subsidy. DSH cuts reduce Medicaid supplemental payments to skilled nursing facilities alongside acute-care hospitals. Work requirements convert Medicaid patients to uninsured patients in both settings.

Both policies hit Omega's tenants at once.

The Court Delays and State Fixes That Have Not Closed the Gap

Legal challenges to the OBBBA's Medicaid work requirement provisions are active in multiple states. If courts issue broad injunctions before the December implementation deadline, the 6 million coverage-loss projection will come in lower. Several states have sought implementation delays, and the administrative infrastructure required to verify work compliance is, in many states, not yet fully built.

A second scenario that limits damage is aggressive cost management by large for-profit hospital chains. Tenet raised its 2026 guidance after the second quarter despite the DSH cut beginning eight months earlier. HCA has consistently reduced costs faster than reimbursement declines. If that pattern holds through 2026, the equity impact may be concentrated in the hospital bond market and REIT sector rather than hitting publicly traded hospital operating company stocks directly.

The third potential offset is state-level supplemental payment programs. Several states are designing alternative Medicaid supplemental structures to replace the DSH program. Those programs require CMS approval. The agency has indicated approval timelines of 18 to 24 months. No state replacement program that could offset the October 2025 cut has been approved.

The legal path buys time. The cost-cutting path concentrates the damage in specific sectors rather than eliminating it. The state replacement path does not yet exist.

The Uncompensated Care Line That Grows Each Quarter

The financial statements that will fully reflect the combined impact of DSH cuts and Medicaid enrollment losses have not yet been published. Coverage losses are still ramping through state systems. The DSH cut completed its first full fiscal year in September 2026.

The uncompensated care cost figures in HCA's and Tenet's quarterly filings are backward-looking. They capture patients who arrived, received treatment, and could not pay. The 6 million people in the process of losing Medicaid coverage will not all appear in those figures until 2027. By then, the hospital bond market may already be repricing the sector.

It moves in one direction.

Plain English

Even though hospital companies whose stocks you can buy appear to be reporting solid earnings, with Tenet raising its guidance and HCA posting billions in quarterly revenue, the financial supports underneath the system are being cut away. I argue this because the federal government simultaneously removed more than half of the payments specifically designed to compensate hospitals for treating people who cannot pay, and made it far harder for people to qualify for Medicaid in the first place, meaning more patients will arrive at emergency rooms with no coverage at all. Although large hospital chains have managed their costs well enough to absorb the first rounds of these changes for now, the full impact has not yet appeared in their financial statements, because the millions of people losing Medicaid coverage are still working through state bureaucratic systems. You may start seeing higher costs for your own health insurance as hospitals shift uncompensated care costs onto private payers, and emergency rooms may become harder to reach in communities that depend on safety-net facilities that can no longer afford to stay open.
