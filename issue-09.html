America’s Most Watched Gas Metric Was Calibrated Before the LNG Boom — Now It’s Wrong

US natural gas storage sits above its five-year average entering winter, but that average was built when the US exported half as much gas. Three new terminals changed the demand structure permanently. The price has not caught up.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  October 13, 2026  ·  9:36 AM ET

Why 68 Billion Cubic Feet Above Average Is Not the Cushion It Looks Like

US natural gas storage stood at 3,500 billion cubic feet (Bcf) as of October 2. That is 68 Bcf above the five-year average for the same week. On the metric every gas trader watches, the market is heading into winter with an adequate buffer.

It isn't.

The five-year average is a rolling calculation using weekly inventory data from 2022 through 2026. In 2022, US liquefied natural gas exports averaged roughly 11 billion cubic feet per day (Bcf/d). By the first half of 2026, that figure had reached 17.4 Bcf/d, a 58% increase. The averaging window was constructed during years when domestic supply faced a structurally lower export pull.

When gas is being drawn out of the domestic system 6 Bcf/d faster than the baseline assumed, a storage level that read comfortable in 2022 terms represents tighter conditions today. The gas in storage does not care what year the average was calculated. It drains at whatever rate demand requires. And demand now includes a global market.

Three Terminals Rewired the Demand Floor in Eighteen Months

The US Energy Information Administration puts US peak LNG export capacity at 18.3 Bcf/d as of October 2026. Three facilities drove the step change.

Plaquemines LNG, the largest new US export terminal, is running at full capacity. Corpus Christi Stage 3 is operating from six of its seven liquefaction trains. Golden Pass shipped its first cargo on April 22, 2026; its second train is scheduled to come online before year-end, adding approximately 0.7 Bcf/d of capacity.

These terminals do not slow when US domestic prices tick up or when the economy softens. They run at whatever rate global LNG demand supports, and global LNG demand is set by prices in Asia and Europe, not by Henry Hub. When Asian LNG benchmarks are running three to four times above the domestic US price, every available unit of Appalachian and Gulf Coast gas flows to export first. Storage injection gets whatever is left. The EIA projects exports of 17.3 Bcf/d through year-end 2026, rising to 18.7 Bcf/d in the first half of 2027. Three more terminals are under construction. The demand floor is rising every quarter, and the five-year average has not kept pace.

Who Absorbs the Cost When Henry Hub Moves

Two categories of publicly traded companies carry underpriced exposure to a domestic gas price correction.

Regulated gas utilities, including NiSource, Atmos Energy, and Spire, purchase gas at market prices and deliver it to customers at rates set by state utility commissions. When input costs rise sharply, rate adjustment filings take 12 to 24 months to complete. A spike in Henry Hub this November would not reach customer bills until late 2027 at the earliest. In that gap, the utility absorbs the difference. None of these companies carry earnings estimates that assume materially higher gas costs through the winter.

Nitrogen fertilizer producers carry more immediate exposure. CF Industries and its peers use natural gas as the primary feedstock for anhydrous ammonia, the base compound for most nitrogen fertilizers. Natural gas represents more than two-thirds of CF Industries' production cost. The company hedges a portion of its exposure, but unhedged production runs at whatever Henry Hub price prevails. A meaningful upward move in gas prices compresses margins with no offset. CF Industries stock has been trading near its trailing average. The earnings estimate embedded in that price assumes modest, stable gas costs through 2027.

Why Appalachian Producers Are Still Priced for a Domestic Commodity

Antero Resources has described itself as the highest-LNG-exposure producer among Appalachian operators. In the first quarter of 2026, the company sold 2.3 Bcf/d of production into sales points along the LNG export corridor and realized an average natural gas price of $5.57 per thousand cubic feet before hedges, against a benchmark index price of $5.04. The premium comes from direct proximity to export demand centers.

Antero management cited approximately $300 million in projected margin improvement through 2028 from that LNG-linked positioning, with the potential to expand toward $600 to $700 million over five years as its gas sales mix shifts further toward export-fairway pricing. EQT Corporation, the largest US natural gas producer, operates under a similar structural advantage from its Appalachian base near LNG-corridor pipelines.

Both companies' stock prices have tracked Henry Hub movements rather than global LNG benchmarks. That pricing logic will hold until enough transactions clear at export-parity prices that analysts reprice enterprise values for a globally connected market. That repricing has not happened yet. The producers are valued like regional commodity sellers. The infrastructure says otherwise.

What Keeps Gas at Three Dollars

Three scenarios limit the upside.

A warm winter matters most. Heating degree days running well below the 30-year average reduce residential consumption and extend the injection season into early November. End-of-season storage above 3,600 Bcf would reduce winter draw risk materially. The La Nina weather pattern, if sustained, raises that probability.

LNG facility outages are possible. Plaquemines, Corpus Christi Stage 3, and Golden Pass Train 2 are all in their first full production cycles. Unplanned mechanical downtime returns export gas to the domestic market and adds to storage, cutting into the arbitrage. The risk is non-trivial with new equipment.

Appalachian production can also surprise upward. The basin has constrained capacity that becomes economic above $4 per MMBtu. Producer discipline has held at current prices. A sustained move higher would bring incremental supply online within 60 to 90 days, capping how far prices can run before new volumes arrive.

The Benchmark That Gets Restated When the Window Moves

The five-year storage average advances by one year each January. When 2027 arrives, 2022 data drops out of the calculation window. In 2022, US LNG exports averaged roughly 11 Bcf/d. When that year exits, the average baseline incorporates a period with substantially higher structural export demand. What looks like a 68 Bcf surplus today may print as a deficit against the updated baseline by spring 2027, without a single additional unit of gas entering or leaving storage.

The EIA has noted the shift directly. Its forecasts project storage gradually moving below the rolling five-year average as export capacity grows. The agency publishes that forecast. The futures market has not responded to it.

Henry Hub's November 2026 contract settled at $3.22 per MMBtu on October 9. Morgan Stanley's winter bull case is $5. The gap between those two numbers is the market treating US natural gas as a domestic commodity priced against domestic supply and demand. One of those treatments is correct. Three new export terminals shipped their first cargoes this year. The market has not yet decided which world it is in.

Plain English

Even though US natural gas supplies look healthy right now, with storage sitting slightly above its historical average heading into winter, that average was built during years when the US was exporting far less gas abroad than it does today. I argue this because three major export terminals started operations in the last 18 months, and the US is now shipping natural gas to Asia and Europe at levels close to double what they were four years ago, pulling domestic supply toward global prices in a way the old comparison numbers never accounted for. Although this hasn't caused any visible shortage yet, the cushion is thinner than the headline number suggests, and gas prices this winter could come in higher than the current market expects. If that happens, you may start seeing higher heating bills and rising costs for fertilizers and other goods made from natural gas, at a time when most people assumed the supply situation was perfectly under control.
