Japan’s Trillion-Dollar Exit — The Largest Holder of U.S. Debt Is Selling

Japan holds more U.S. Treasuries than any other country, and for the first time in three decades its life insurers and government are both actively selling, a structural withdrawal that equity markets are treating as noise.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  September 18, 2026  ·  9:21 AM ET

Why the World’s Most Reliable Treasury Buyer Stopped Buying

Japan holds $1.12 trillion in U.S. Treasury bonds, more than any other country on earth. For roughly three decades, that capital flowed steadily from Tokyo into Washington because the arithmetic made sense: domestic Japanese yields sat near zero or below, American yields were higher, and currency hedging costs were manageable. Japanese life insurers became the single most consistent source of non-U.S. demand for American debt.

They aren’t. Not anymore.

The Bank of Japan spent the past two years normalizing interest rates after a decade of near-zero and negative rate policy. At a 30-year Japanese government bond auction on September 3, 2026, yields cleared above 4 percent, up from below 1.5 percent in early 2023. For the first time in a generation, Japanese institutions can earn competitive returns at home without taking on dollar currency risk. The hedged spread between U.S. Treasuries and domestic government bonds that drove three decades of cross-border capital flows has effectively closed.

Japan’s government made the shift explicit with its own actions. In August 2026, Tokyo’s Ministry of Finance reported that Japan’s foreign reserve holdings fell by $87.8 billion in a single month, as it sold assets to fund the largest currency intervention in Japanese history. The United States joined the operation, making it the first joint U.S.-Japan yen-buying intervention since 1998, with the two governments spending a combined $98.6 billion to defend a yen that had weakened to 164 per dollar, its weakest level in roughly 40 years.

When Washington agrees to help an allied government liquidate American bonds, the signal is not subtle.

The Bank of Japan’s Rate Hike Created $194 Billion in Trapped Losses

Japan’s life insurance industry spent decades buying domestic government bonds when yields sat at near zero or below. Nippon Life, Dai-ichi Life, Sumitomo Life, and Meiji Yasuda loaded balance sheets with Japanese government bonds that paid almost nothing in nominal terms, because those bonds still outperformed deflation-era domestic alternatives.

That era ended abruptly.

Japan’s major life insurers reported combined unrealized losses of 30.86 trillion yen, approximately $194 billion, on their domestic bond portfolios as of June 2026. That figure is 60 percent higher than a year earlier. The four largest firms alone reported 15.13 trillion yen in unrealized losses, roughly $96 billion, as 30-year Japanese government bond yields moved from below 1.5 percent to above 4 percent in under three years.

These losses remain unrealized because insurers plan to hold bonds to maturity and match long-term policy liabilities. But intention and capacity diverge when policyholders begin surrendering. Sony Life Insurance’s policy lapse and surrender rate rose 0.2 percentage points year over year, reaching 1.4 percent in the April-June 2026 quarter. T&D Financial Life’s surrender rate climbed 0.88 percentage points to 1.56 percent. Each policy surrender requires the insurer to liquidate assets to fund the redemption. Paper losses become realized ones.

Japan’s Financial Services Agency has begun formally questioning the major insurers about their bond positions. When regulators start asking questions, they are typically one step behind the problem.

The Sectors Still Priced on 3 Percent Rate Assumptions

The U.S. 10-year Treasury yield was trading at 4.94 percent on September 17, 2026, near its highest level since 2007. That level reflects multiple reinforcing pressures: record U.S. government borrowing, a Federal Reserve rate hike in 2026, and reduced foreign demand from Japan and others. The 10-year is not rising because the economy is overheating. It is rising because the buyers who used to absorb supply at these levels are no longer there.

The transmission to equity markets runs through the discount rate. Higher long-term yields increase the rate at which future corporate earnings are discounted back to present value. Growth stocks carry the most exposure because their valuations depend most heavily on earnings years in the future. Analysts estimate that every 100-basis-point rise in the 10-year historically compresses growth multiples by 15 to 20 percent. Technology valuations have absorbed some of this adjustment, but a broad cohort of growth equities remains priced on discount rate assumptions from a lower-rate era.

Real estate investment trusts face compression from two directions. They borrow at rates tied to Treasury benchmarks and pay out the bulk of earnings as dividends. A 5 percent risk-free rate raises their cost of capital directly and creates genuine competition for income-seeking investors who can now earn that rate without taking equity risk. The REIT sector’s earnings models were calibrated when the 10-year sat near 3.5 percent. That number is now academic.

When Surrender Rates Rise, the Selling Gets Structural

Japan’s life insurers hold foreign bond portfolios, including U.S. Treasuries, partly as diversification from domestic bond exposure. But the economics of holding those foreign bonds have shifted in both directions.

The cost of hedging yen-dollar currency risk has risen sharply as U.S. and Japanese interest rates have diverged. Currency-hedged U.S. Treasuries now yield less than equivalent domestic Japanese bonds for a Japanese insurer. Unhedged U.S. Treasuries expose institutions to yen appreciation risk that becomes a realized loss if the Bank of Japan’s normalization strengthens the currency further. Neither option is economically attractive.

Meiji Yasuda stated publicly it would consider buying U.S. Treasuries only if yields rise significantly and hedging costs decline. Nippon Life reported cutting its foreign bond allocation in its most recent portfolio disclosure. These are not tactical portfolio shifts. Japan’s life insurers represent hundreds of billions of dollars in long-duration fixed income demand that acted as a persistent price floor for U.S. Treasuries across multiple rate cycles. Their withdrawal does not arrive as a single large sell order. It arrives as a persistent absence of the bids that used to be there.

The compounding effect runs through rising surrender rates. As Japanese policyholders exit low-returning insurance products in favor of higher-yielding domestic alternatives, insurers must sell assets to fund the redemptions. The assets most likely to be sold are the foreign bonds that no longer make economic sense to hold. That selling places additional supply pressure on U.S. Treasuries at precisely the moment when U.S. fiscal needs require the market to absorb record issuance volumes.

Three Conditions That Would Stop the Selling Pressure

The thesis has three legitimate failure paths.

If the Bank of Japan reverses course, pausing or cutting its normalization program in response to financial stability concerns, domestic Japanese government bond yields would fall, unrealized insurer losses would shrink, and the pressure on balance sheets would ease. The BOJ has shown sensitivity to market disruption; the August intervention was partly motivated by concern that a rapidly weakening yen was destabilizing the broader financial system. A material slowdown in Japan’s domestic economy could prompt a policy reversal within a few quarters.

If U.S. yields fall independently, through a domestic economic slowdown or a shift in Federal Reserve forward guidance, the hedged spread between Japanese government bonds and U.S. Treasuries could widen back in favor of American bonds. That would revive the economics of Japanese institutional buying and potentially produce a significant bid for U.S. duration that would relieve yield pressure in both directions.

The third scenario is the one already demonstrated: direct bilateral policy coordination. The joint U.S.-Japan August intervention shows both governments treat Japanese capital flows as a shared problem. A further escalation in yield pressure, toward levels the U.S. Treasury views as fiscally destabilizing, could trigger another coordinated response. That would cap yields in the short term. Who buys American debt after Japan steps back is a harder problem to solve.

Plain English

Even though U.S. stock prices look broadly stable and there has been no sudden financial crisis, one of the quiet forces that kept American borrowing costs low for thirty years is no longer doing its job. I argue this because Japan, the single largest foreign owner of U.S. government bonds, is now selling rather than buying, both because its own insurance companies are sitting on nearly $200 billion in losses from rising interest rates at home and because the Japanese government sold nearly $90 billion in foreign assets in a single month just to stop its currency from falling further. Although this has not yet caused a sudden disruption, it is part of why the rate the U.S. government pays to borrow has stayed stubbornly high. When government borrowing costs stay high, so do borrowing costs for companies and homeowners, and the value of stocks that depend on cheap money tends to fall over months rather than days, even without a single dramatic event to point to.
