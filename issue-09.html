Three Years Came Up Short — America’s Power Deficit Is Getting Priced Into Every Generator Contract

PJM has failed to procure enough power three consecutive years while data center demand outpaces what the interconnection queue can deliver, and the generators already on the grid are charging accordingly.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  September 1, 2026  ·  9:39 AM ET

The Auction That Hit Its Price Cap and Still Came Up Short

PJM Interconnection manages electricity delivery for 65 million people across 13 states and Washington DC. It is the largest grid operator in the United States. In July 2025, it released results from its annual capacity auction covering the 2026 to 2027 delivery year.

The clearing price was $329.17 per megawatt-day. That is the legal price cap, the maximum the auction is permitted to pay generators. It was also a record, up from the prior year's record of $269.92 per megawatt-day. Two delivery years earlier, the same auction cleared at $28.92.

Price caps exist to prevent market manipulation. They were not designed to be the market-clearing price. PJM hit the cap anyway.

The $329.17 price produced $16.1 billion in total capacity payments to generators, up 9.5% from the prior year's $14.7 billion. Then the grid still needed more. In December 2025, PJM held a supplemental auction for the 2027 to 2028 delivery year. It came up 6,625 megawatts short of its reliability target, the first time in the grid's history it failed to procure enough capacity. The 2028 to 2029 auction repeated the failure: another 6,831 megawatt shortfall, at a new cap of $333.44 per megawatt-day.

PJM has now missed its reliability target three consecutive years. The market is not pricing a temporary disruption. It is pricing a structural condition.

Two Thousand Six Hundred Gigawatts in a Queue That Takes Five Years to Clear

There are 2,600 gigawatts of energy projects nationally waiting to connect to the US grid as of 2026. Total US generating capacity currently online is approximately 1,200 gigawatts. The queue holds more than twice the existing grid.

Most of it will never connect. Nationally, about one in four projects that enter the interconnection queue reaches commercial operation. In PJM, more than 800 projects totaling roughly 220 gigawatts of capacity applied under the grid's reformed interconnection process. In all of 2025, 3.045 gigawatts came online. In 2025 alone, 38 gigawatts of projects withdrew from the PJM queue after a single deposit requirement change exposed how many applicants were placeholder filings rather than committed projects.

The median period from interconnection application to commercial operation is approaching five years. In MISO, covering the Midwest, the backlog holds 170 gigawatts with a four-year average wait time. A project that clears every approval on schedule still faces years of transmission studies, equipment procurement, and construction before it delivers a single watt.

That timeline cannot absorb demand that arrives in months.

PJM's own load forecast projects peak demand growth of 32 gigawatts from 2024 to 2030. Thirty of those gigawatts come from data centers. The grid's internal analysis attributes 94% of the projected demand increase in its service territory to data center load growth. Data center electricity consumption in the United States was 61.8 gigawatts in 2025 and is projected to reach 75.8 gigawatts in 2026 and 134.4 gigawatts by 2030. From 2027 through 2032, PJM expects data centers to add 5 to 7 gigawatts of new demand per year while new supply additions run at 2 to 3 gigawatts per year.

The gap between those two numbers grows every year.

Constellation, Vistra, and Talen Are Billing the Shortage

Capacity auction payments flow to generators whose plants are already on the grid. Constellation Energy cleared 18,875 megawatts of capacity in the 2028 to 2029 auction at a weighted average price near $325 per megawatt-day, covering 15,700 megawatts of nuclear output and 3,175 megawatts of fossil and other units. Vistra cleared approximately 10,924 megawatts at the same price. Both stocks registered single-session gains when auction results published.

Talen Energy negotiated a different structure. It committed to supply 1,920 megawatts of carbon-free nuclear power from its Susquehanna plant in Pennsylvania to Amazon Web Services under a 17-year power purchase agreement with total projected revenue of $18 billion. Amazon is paying for power it cannot build fast enough to secure through the interconnection process. The deal is not a convenience arrangement. It is a premium payment for a resource with no available substitute at this timescale.

The economics work because existing generators are not competing against new entrants. New entrants cannot arrive in time. A buyer that needs 1,920 megawatts of dispatchable, carbon-free power by 2029 cannot source it from the interconnection queue. Talen holds the only asset that delivers it.

That is what $18 billion in contracted revenue looks like when the queue is five years long.

When Data Centers Add Seven Gigawatts and the Grid Adds Three

FERC issued orders in June 2026 directing grid operators to revise their interconnection processes within 60 days to address data center load growth. The orders covered queue management, cost allocation, and large-load treatment. They did not change the minimum time required to build a power plant.

A new combined-cycle gas unit takes 4 to 5 years from permitting to first power. A new nuclear unit takes longer. Even a project that clears the interconnection queue on the first attempt still faces multi-year construction timelines. The 2,600 gigawatt national queue does not represent 2,600 gigawatts of ready-to-build projects. Most applicants lack permits, committed equipment, or financing.

Equipment availability is a secondary constraint closing in on the primary one. Vistra's management noted in its 2026 earnings commentary that costs for some generation equipment have doubled or tripled. Power transformer lead times now extend to 3 to 4 years for some configurations. The supply chain for generation equipment did not scale when interconnection applications did. Regulatory acceleration helps queue management. It does not shorten transformer delivery times.

What breaks the shortage is a sustained wave of new generation that has to be built, financed, and connected at a rate the supply chain cannot currently support.

The Three Numbers That Tell You How Long This Lasts

Three metrics carry the most signal. First, the clearing price in PJM's 2029 to 2030 capacity auction, expected in mid-2027. If prices clear at or near the $333 cap again, the market is confirming the shortage extends through at least 2030. Second, power transformer and major equipment lead times. Transformers are the binding constraint on generation interconnection schedules. When lead times compress, new supply can accelerate into the queue. Third, the terms of new power purchase agreements between generators and hyperscale buyers. The implied per-megawatt-hour pricing in the Talen-Amazon deal reflects a scarcity premium. When that premium narrows in subsequent disclosed deals, buyers have meaningful alternatives again.

The direction of those three numbers tells you whether the structural condition is deepening or resolving.

If the AI Buildout Slows Before the Grid Catches Up

The demand side is the thesis. If data center construction decelerates sharply, the 5 to 7 gigawatt annual demand additions that PJM projects shrink materially. A sustained pullback in AI infrastructure investment would remove the primary driver of the load growth forecast. The capacity shortfall would ease faster than supply additions alone could manage it.

A FERC-ordered reduction in the capacity auction price caps would change the earnings math for generators without changing the underlying supply-demand position. Generators would receive less per megawatt-day, but the shortage would persist. That outcome is possible but has not been proposed.

Full normalization of generation equipment supply chains would pull forward construction timelines on projects already in the queue, allowing supply to reach the grid faster than current projections allow. That is the most plausible path to supply relief. It depends on transformer manufacturers, not grid operators.

The Shortfall Grew Again at the Next Auction

The capacity price went from $28.92 to $333.44 in two delivery years. Each auction that hits the cap is a signal that the existing price ceiling does not reflect what the market would actually pay. The grid operator fell short of its reliability target for a third consecutive year, and the generators that cleared capacity at record prices will collect the difference between a $333 ceiling and whatever the uncapped price would have been.

The queue is not a temporary bottleneck. It is the structure of the market.

Plain English

Even though the lights are staying on and there have been no widespread blackouts, the system that makes sure there is enough electricity available every day has failed to secure enough power three years in a row. I argue this because there is a massive line of new power projects waiting to connect to the grid, and that line now takes about five years to clear, while the data centers being built for AI need electricity connections that are ready now, not in five years. Although the grid has held together for now, the companies that already own working power plants are in a position to charge record prices because no new competition can arrive fast enough to undercut them. You may start seeing this show up in higher electricity bills, particularly in the mid-Atlantic and Midwest states where most of the data center construction is concentrated, because the extra cost the grid is paying for power eventually gets passed down to homes and businesses.
