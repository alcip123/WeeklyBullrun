The IRA Price Clock — Medicare’s Drug Negotiation Just Hit Ozempic, and the Program Only Gets Bigger From Here

Ozempic faces a 71 percent Medicare price cut starting January 2027, but the Inflation Reduction Act mandates 15 new drug negotiations per year through 2028 and 20 thereafter, and analyst revenue models for the pharma companies next in line have not adjusted for it.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  October 16, 2026  ·  9:14 AM ET

Ozempic is the best-selling prescription drug in the United States. Starting January 1, 2027, Medicare will pay $277 per month for it. The current list price is $959.

That is a 71 percent reduction imposed by federal negotiation. The company that makes it confirmed acceptance. The drug will not be pulled from the market.

This is not an isolated event. It is the second round of a program designed to repeat indefinitely, and the next round is already underway.

How a 71 Percent Medicare Price Cut on the World’s Biggest Drug Gets Absorbed Without a Headline

The Inflation Reduction Act authorized Medicare to negotiate drug prices directly with manufacturers for the first time. The structure is deliberate: ten drugs in the first round, prices effective January 2026; fifteen drugs in the second round, effective January 2027; fifteen in the third round, effective January 2028; twenty drugs per year starting in 2029.

Round 2 final prices were published in late 2025. The fifteen Part D drugs included Ozempic, Wegovy, and Rybelsus, all three of which contain Novo Nordisk’s semaglutide. CMS set the maximum fair price for Ozempic at $277 per month, down from its list price of approximately $959. Wegovy was set at roughly $386 for its highest available dose. Novo Nordisk confirmed both prices and accepted the terms.

The gross Medicare Part D spending on the fifteen Round 2 drugs totaled approximately $27 billion in the twelve months before their selection, covering 1.8 million Medicare beneficiaries. CMS projects Medicare and its beneficiaries will collectively save approximately $12 billion annually once the new prices apply.

Novo separately announced it would reduce list prices starting in 2027: approximately 35 percent for Ozempic and approximately 50 percent for Wegovy. List price reductions are not limited to Medicare. Commercial insurers and pharmacy benefit managers use list prices as the starting point for their own reimbursement negotiations. A 35 to 50 percent list price cut resets the revenue per prescription across every payer class simultaneously.

The Ratchet in the Law: Each Round Covers More Drugs and a Larger Portion of the Market

The program does not run to exhaustion. It expands.

CMS announced the Round 3 drug list in January 2026, covering fifteen new drugs to be priced by 2028. For the first time, Round 3 includes drugs covered under Medicare Part B, the benefit that pays for drugs administered in clinical settings: intravenous cancer treatments, infused biologics for autoimmune disease, and physician-administered specialty medications. Part B drugs include some of the highest per-treatment costs in all of medicine. Including them for the first time extends the negotiation program into the most expensive corner of the pharmaceutical market.

From 2029 onward, the law mandates twenty drugs per year. The selection criteria focus on total Medicare expenditure. That framework is transparent enough that the pipeline is largely predictable: the drugs most exposed to future rounds are the ones generating the largest Medicare revenues today.

Eli Lilly’s tirzepatide, marketed as Mounjaro for diabetes and Zepbound for obesity, is the fastest-growing drug in the US market. Lilly has guided toward annual US revenues from tirzepatide that could reach $25 billion. At that revenue scale, Medicare spending on Mounjaro would qualify it for early selection in a subsequent round. Lilly’s equity multiple is built on revenue projections that do not embed a mandatory negotiated price reduction.

The Company Whose Entire Valuation Depends on the Drug That Was Just Negotiated

Novo Nordisk’s market capitalization tracks almost exclusively to its GLP-1 franchise. Ozempic, Wegovy, and Rybelsus collectively accounted for approximately 77 percent of total company revenue in 2024. No major pharmaceutical company is more concentrated in a single drug family.

Medicare Part D covers approximately 60 million Americans. GLP-1 therapies are heavily prescribed in the Medicare population because the prevalence of type 2 diabetes among adults over 65 is approximately 27 percent, according to CDC estimates. Of the 1.8 million beneficiaries using the fifteen Round 2 drugs before negotiation, a significant share were Ozempic and Wegovy patients.

The combined effect of the Medicare negotiated price and the voluntary list price cut creates a permanent repricing. A drug that generated revenue at $959 per month will generate revenue at $277 per month from Medicare, and at a 35 percent discount from list on commercial payers starting in January 2027. Neither number is a temporary promotion. Both are structural resets with no scheduled reversal.

Novo has stated that expanded access at lower prices will bring more patients into treatment and partially offset the revenue impact. The company has not provided a public model showing how many incremental prescriptions at $277 would be needed to match the lost revenue from the price differential on existing volume. Analyst consensus for NVO still reflects revenue trajectories derived from 2024 and 2025 pricing.

What the Acceleration Means for Every Other Pharma Revenue Model

The standard pharmaceutical valuation model discounts a drug’s revenue stream over its expected patent life. For high-spend Medicare drugs, the IRA installs a mandatory price reduction event inside that cash flow model before patent expiry. Round 1 established the mechanism. Round 2 applied it to the best-selling drug class in the United States. Round 3 extends it to Part B for the first time.

Merck’s Keytruda is the world’s top-selling cancer drug, generating approximately $22 billion in annual global revenue. It is a Part B drug administered in clinical settings, placing it directly in scope for future Part B negotiation rounds. Merck’s valuation model depends on Keytruda’s revenue trajectory through the late 2020s. That model does not appear to embed a negotiated Medicare price reduction.

AstraZeneca and Pfizer each have drugs already in Round 1 negotiations and portfolios that qualify for future selection under the same criteria. Bristol-Myers Squibb’s Eliquis was negotiated in Round 1. Its remaining high-spend drugs sit in the pipeline for future rounds.

The equity implication is systematic: the IRA creates a mandatory price reduction event for every drug generating significant Medicare expenditure, including drugs still under active patent protection. That event was not present in pharmaceutical valuation models before 2022. It now affects every drug above a Medicare spending threshold, on a published schedule, with no expiration.

When Volume Growth Actually Offsets the Price Cut

The case for the bullish pharma thesis rests on access expansion.

Negotiated prices dramatically lower out-of-pocket costs for Medicare beneficiaries, potentially drawing patients into treatment who previously could not afford full-price prescriptions. The addressable population for GLP-1 therapy is large: approximately 38 million Americans have type 2 diabetes and an estimated 100 million meet obesity criteria. Expanded Medicare coverage at lower copays could add significant prescription volume.

Some analysts have characterized the net effect on Novo as manageable. BMO noted that volume gains from broader access could offset revenue headwinds from lower per-dose pricing. Those notes have not quantified a break-even prescription volume. Without that figure, the volume thesis is an assumption built on a mechanism that is real but unmodeled.

The round that just closed negotiated the world’s biggest drug down 71 percent. The one opening now covers the drugs that follow.

Plain English

Even though the government has been publicly announcing discounts on popular prescription drugs, the story has been framed almost entirely as good news for patients, rather than as a problem for the companies that make those drugs. I argue this because the law that authorized these negotiations requires the process to continue and expand every year, meaning the price cuts are not a one-time adjustment but a permanent program that will reach more and more drugs until it covers the most profitable medicines in the country. Although drug companies have said that lower prices may attract more patients and offset some lost revenue, they have not shown the math behind that claim. You may start seeing this show up as slower earnings growth at major pharmaceutical companies over the next several years, which could affect the value of retirement accounts or mutual funds that hold large positions in companies like Novo Nordisk, Merck, or Eli Lilly.
