Bond Investors Are Buying Hospital Debt at Record Pace — And Ignoring What October Does to the Revenue

Nonprofit hospitals issued $41.3 billion in bonds in 2025, the largest borrowing year on record, while 39 percent of the sector was already losing money; the Medicaid eligibility changes taking effect this October remove the revenue assumption that justified every one of those bonds.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  September 8, 2026  ·  9:32 AM ET

The Record Bond Year That Masks a Weakening Sector

Nonprofit hospitals had their best borrowing year on record in 2025. They also had more credit downgrades than upgrades.

Hospital long-term bond issuance reached $41.3 billion in 2025, a 22 percent jump from the $33.5 billion record set in 2024. Hospitals now account for 7 percent of total municipal bond issuance, well above the 10-year average of 5.4 percent. Rating agencies assigned the sector stable or neutral outlooks. Bond investors kept buying.

Beneath the record issuance, 39 percent of hospitals were already running operating losses before any federal policy change. S&P Global Ratings downgraded 41 hospital systems in 2025 while upgrading only 30, producing an upgrade-to-downgrade ratio of 0.73. Fitch described the sector as split into three groups: the top 20 percent using strong balance sheets to grow, the middle 65 percent holding steady, and the bottom 15 percent facing accelerating deterioration.

The bottom 15 percent are about to lose their primary revenue source.

Why a Credit Downgrade at a Nonprofit Hospital Is Not Like One at a Public Company

Unlike investor-owned hospital chains, nonprofit systems cannot issue equity. Their only path to external capital runs through bond markets. Every expansion, every equipment upgrade, every technology investment is funded by a credit rating.

When margins compress, the response is binary. A nonprofit hospital facing a cash squeeze cannot dilute shareholders or sell convertible notes. It can cut costs or it can borrow at whatever rate its current credit rating permits. A downgrade raises the cost of new borrowing and can trigger covenant provisions in existing bond agreements. For a system already operating at margins below 2 percent, a 25 basis point increase in debt service cost is a material event.

Children's Hospital Los Angeles is already there. Moody's downgraded it to Ba2 in 2026, below investment grade, citing challenged operations and heavy reliance on Medicaid funding. It will not be the last.

The $41.3 billion in bonds issued in 2025 priced in a federal funding structure that no longer exists.

Medtronic's $35 Billion Revenue Relies on Capital Budgets That Are About to Freeze

The equity market has not connected Medicaid policy to medical device earnings. It should.

When hospitals face margin compression, capital equipment is the first expense deferred. It is discretionary, postponable, and large. A hospital system can delay a new MRI suite or surgical robot upgrade by two to four quarters without immediate patient impact. It cannot defer payroll or bond payments.

Medtronic has been flagged as high risk by analysts studying its exposure to hospital capital spending. Its fiscal 2026 revenue guidance stands at $35.7 to $35.8 billion, with management expecting approximately 5.5 percent growth. That projection assumes hospital capital budgets remain broadly intact. Stryker and Becton Dickinson carry similar exposure across surgical devices and diagnostic equipment.

Safety-net hospitals nationwide are projected to see combined net operating income fall by $5 billion as Medicaid changes phase in. A $5 billion income loss across institutions already running thin margins does not produce equipment purchase orders.

When the October Eligibility Changes Hit Systems Already Running on Negative Margins

The One Big Beautiful Bill cuts approximately $625 billion from Medicaid over ten years. The Congressional Budget Office estimates 7.8 million people will lose coverage. The equity market has tracked these numbers as a policy debate. It has not priced them as a hospital credit event.

The cuts arrive in sequence. Federal incentives for states to maintain Medicaid expansion ended in January 2026. On October 1, eligibility rules for immigrants narrow significantly, removing a material share of current Medicaid-enrolled patients from hospital revenue. By December, eligibility redeterminations shift from every 12 months to every 6, meaning coverage lapses and uncompensated care conversions become continuous rather than episodic.

Each uninsured patient treated in an emergency department becomes uncompensated care. The American Hospital Association estimated that uncompensated care cost hospitals $42 billion in 2023, before any of these changes. That number is set to increase sharply, concentrated at safety-net hospitals where 40 to 60 percent of patients are Medicaid-covered.

The hospitals that issued those 2025 bonds will be filing 2026 financial statements with materially lower revenue.

Three Signals That Will Move Before the Stock Prices Do

First, the S&P hospital bond downgrade ratio in the fourth quarter of 2026. Downgrades already exceeded upgrades in 2025, before the major eligibility changes took effect. S&P releases periodic healthcare sector reviews; a ratio widening past the 2025 level of 0.73 is the earliest observable signal that the credit deterioration is accelerating.

Second, Medtronic's capital equipment order data in its November 2026 earnings call. Medtronic reports its fiscal first quarter in late November. If hospital customers are deferring large equipment orders, the shift appears in backlog figures before it shows up in revenue. Analysts rarely track backlog as a leading indicator for this sector.

Third, credit spreads on hospital municipal bonds. In a Bond Buyer survey conducted in 2026, 72 percent of municipal market participants said a funding crisis in the healthcare sector was likely. When spreads on hospital bonds widen ahead of formal rating actions, medical device stocks have historically followed within two to three quarters.

If States Fill the Revenue Gap Before the Downgrades Arrive

Medicaid is jointly funded by states and the federal government. Several expansion states, including New York, California, and Massachusetts, have both the political will and in some cases the fiscal capacity to increase state-level contributions and partially offset the federal cuts. If states compensate at meaningful scale, the hospital revenue shock is smaller than the projections suggest.

This thesis also breaks if hospitals shift procedures to ambulatory surgery centers faster than the base case assumes. That migration is already underway and is structurally irreversible. A faster shift could stabilize margins at mid-tier systems even as Medicaid revenue declines, reducing the capital budget pressure that flows to device companies.

A third exit: rating agencies could grant hospitals more time to demonstrate cost responses before issuing formal downgrades. Bond market contagion requires official rating actions to trigger. If agencies are slower than their methodology implies, the credit event is delayed and medical device companies absorb the revenue impact in 2027 rather than the second half of 2026.

Seven Million Patients and the Bond Ratings Behind Them

Seven point eight million people losing Medicaid is a policy story. For Medtronic and Stryker shareholders, it is an earnings story.

Plain English

Even though hospitals are reporting near-record borrowing activity and most major health systems appear financially stable, a quiet funding pressure is building underneath. I argue this because nonprofit hospitals cannot raise money by selling shares the way regular companies can -- they can only borrow, and those loans were priced on Medicaid revenue that Congress is now cutting by $625 billion over the next ten years, with the first major eligibility changes rolling in this October. Although the large, well-capitalized health systems have enough reserves to absorb the early impact, the smaller community hospitals and safety-net facilities that serve lower-income patients are likely to lose significant government funding just as they owe payments on last year's record borrowing. If those hospitals start cutting orders for new medical equipment and diagnostic machines, the companies that make them -- selling tens of billions of dollars in devices to hospitals each year -- may begin missing their sales targets in ways that stock prices have not yet reflected.
