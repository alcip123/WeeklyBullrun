The Trillion-Dollar Data Center That Cannot Turn On

Technology companies have committed $650 billion to AI infrastructure in 2026. The power grid they need to turn it on has an eight-year waiting list.
By Alessio Cipriano  ·  October 6, 2026  ·  9:23 AM ET

Half the Capacity Planned for 2026 Is Not Being Built

Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, and Meta committed a combined $650 billion to capital expenditure in 2026, the largest single-year infrastructure spending commitment in the history of any industry. Equity analysts absorbed the number and revised AI revenue estimates upward.

The projects are not being built. Nearly 50 percent of US data center capacity planned for 2026 has been delayed or canceled. Of the 12 gigawatts of AI data center capacity announced for this year, approximately 5 gigawatts is actually under construction. The gap between announcement and groundbreaking is the widest in the industry's history.

The market has not priced this gap.

The bottleneck is not chips. It is not financing. It is not permits. It is the transmission wire connecting a building to the power grid, a physical piece of infrastructure whose approval process now takes longer than the average term of an office lease.

An Eight-Year Line Nobody Priced Into the Model

Grid interconnection is the formal process by which a new large power customer connects to the transmission network. It requires engineering studies, regulatory approvals, infrastructure upgrades, and construction. Before a data center can draw a single watt from the public grid, it must complete this process.

The queue for that process has become a crisis.

The US national interconnection backlog now contains approximately 2,600 gigawatts of proposed projects. The average wait from application to operational connection is five years. In PJM, the regional transmission operator serving the Mid-Atlantic and Midwest including Northern Virginia, the world's largest data center market, the wait for large AI campuses has reached seven to eight years. Texas's ERCOT grid is tracking 474 gigawatts of large-load requests, roughly 90 percent from data centers. One year ago, that number was 63 gigawatts.

The ERCOT queue grew 650 percent in twelve months.

Transformer lead times compound the problem. High-voltage transformers, the equipment that steps power down from transmission lines to usable voltage, now carry lead times of three to five years. Before 2020, the same equipment took 12 to 18 months to procure. The manufacturing base cannot produce grid components fast enough to absorb incoming demand.

The Transformer Bottleneck Behind Every AI Earnings Call

The companies most directly absorbing this constraint are the hyperscalers, but the mechanism runs through three separate groups.

First, the power equipment manufacturers. ABB, Eaton, and Hubbell are running full order books with multiyear backlogs. Their share prices have risen with the broader data center theme. The risk is that order books only convert to revenue if the underlying projects complete. Historically, 30 to 40 percent of queued interconnection projects withdraw before completion. As delays lengthen and project economics deteriorate, that withdrawal rate is rising.

Second, the utilities themselves. Data center demand in PJM's service territory drove a $9.3 billion capacity cost increase in the 2025 to 2026 delivery year. Utilities must invest in grid upgrades to serve new industrial load, capital that earns a regulated return but over a 30 to 40 year depreciation cycle, not the one to two year revenue ramp that AI capex assumptions require.

Third, and most consequential for equity valuations, the hyperscalers. Microsoft abandoned 2 gigawatts of data center projects in the United States and Europe in the first half of 2026. Capital burned on site acquisition, engineering, permitting, and early construction does not appear on any individual income statement line. It shows up later, as write-downs on assets that never operated.

When $650 Billion in Capex Does Not Become Revenue

The equity case for the four largest hyperscalers rests on a specific sequence: capital deployed to data centers produces compute capacity, compute capacity enables AI services, AI services generate revenue that justifies the elevated multiple. That sequence requires the data centers to come online.

The risk is not that the capex fails to materialize. The risk is that it materializes without the corresponding capacity.

Capex that burns in anticipation of a grid connection that never arrives is stranded investment. It sits on the balance sheet as a construction-in-progress asset until it is written down. The impairment appears years after the capital is spent, by which point the revenue expectations it was supposed to support are already embedded in consensus models.

Wall Street AI revenue models for 2027 and 2028 assume that hyperscaler capacity additions track with investment commitments. None of the major sell-side models publicly account for a 50 percent delay rate on announced 2026 capacity. The mismatch between model assumptions and physical build rates is not a tail risk. It is already visible in the data.

The Completion Rate Nobody Is Tracking

Three numbers define how quickly this risk accumulates.

The first is the ratio of announced data center capacity to capacity actually under construction. Five gigawatts under construction against 12 gigawatts announced for 2026 gives a 42 percent completion rate. If this ratio deteriorates as interconnection queues lengthen further, the revenue timeline for AI capex extends by years.

The second is the interconnection withdrawal rate in ERCOT and PJM. Projects that withdraw after spending years in queue represent stranded development cost with no return. Rising withdrawal rates are the most direct indicator of deteriorating project economics.

The third is utility transmission expansion announcements. When a utility files for a multi-billion dollar transmission project, it signals that new large-load capacity will come online in three to five years. That timeline does not match what hyperscaler guidance implies for revenue generation from current capex.

The On-Site Power Bypass and Why It Narrows the Field

This thesis breaks if hyperscalers successfully bypass the interconnection queue through direct power agreements.

Microsoft signed a 20-year contract with Constellation Energy to restart the Unit 1 reactor at Three Mile Island, providing electricity directly to Microsoft facilities without depending on the public grid. Google has contracted for capacity from planned small modular reactor facilities. These agreements, if they produce operational power on schedule, make the interconnection queue irrelevant for the companies that signed them.

The narrowing is the point. The hyperscalers most exposed to grid constraints are not Microsoft, Google, or Amazon. They are second-tier cloud providers, enterprise technology companies building private AI inference infrastructure, and data center operators without the balance sheet to sign decade-long nuclear contracts. For those companies, the public grid is the only path. The queue is their timeline.

The thesis also breaks if FERC Order 2023, adopted in 2023 to reform the interconnection process, accelerates meaningfully. If median wait times compress from eight years to three, the bottleneck becomes less severe. Utilities have been slow to implement the order's requirements, and there is no evidence yet of accelerated processing at scale.

The Queue Gets Longer Every Quarter

Every quarter that passes without meaningful interconnection reform adds to the backlog. New project announcements enter faster than existing projects clear. The ERCOT queue grew 650 percent in one year.

Equity markets are pricing AI infrastructure companies on the assumption that capital deployed converts to operational capacity at the pace management guidance implies. The grid is running on a different schedule.

The queue does not care what the earnings model says.

Plain English

Even though the biggest technology companies in the world are spending hundreds of billions of dollars building the computer infrastructure that powers AI, about half of those data centers are stuck in a waiting line they did not fully account for when they started spending. I argue this because connecting a large building to the power grid in America now takes five to eight years on average, and the electrical equipment needed to make that connection takes another three to five years just to manufacture, which means the bottleneck is not about money or ambition but about physical infrastructure that cannot be built fast enough. Although companies like Microsoft and Google have found workarounds by signing deals directly with nuclear power plants, most other technology companies cannot afford those arrangements. If a data center cannot connect to the power grid, the billions spent on land, engineering, and early construction do not generate any revenue, and the prices investors are paying for these companies today assume that all that spending turns into earnings on a schedule that the power grid cannot actually support.
